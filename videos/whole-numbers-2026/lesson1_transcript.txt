What makes the same digit worth ten times as much?

Its position does.

In our base ten system, ten ones make one ten, and ten tens make one hundred.

Each place is ten times the place immediately to its right.

Look at the seven in this number.

Before I explain, predict which place it holds.

Remember to count from ones at the right.

Look at four hundred and seventy two thousand, six hundred and thirty one.

Put each digit into its own column, from hundred thousands down to ones.

The seven is in the ten thousands column.

Its value is seventy thousand, not seven thousand.

The two stands for two thousand, the six for six hundred, and the three for thirty.

Reading the number in groups helps: four hundred and seventy two thousand, then six hundred and thirty one.

Which one has the greater value?

Point to its column before we compare.

Now compare the two fives in fifty five thousand.

The first five represents fifty thousand.

The second represents five thousand.

Fifty thousand is ten times five thousand, even though the digits look the same.

This relationship helps us multiply and divide whole numbers by ten.

Thirty four times ten is three hundred and forty.

Each digit takes a place worth ten times as much.

Three hundred and forty divided by ten returns thirty four.

A common mix-up is saying that the seven here is just worth seven.

Seven names the digit, but its column tells us its value.

In the ten-thousands column, it represents seven groups of ten thousand.

In 583,214, what is the value of the eight?

The eight is in the ten-thousands place.

Its value is eighty thousand.
