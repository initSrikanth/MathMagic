Can a fraction change its name without changing its size? Today we are exploring equivalent fractions. We will split and regroup equal parts, then check our thinking with pictures. Could one half really be the same amount as four eighths?

Start with one half. Split every part of the whole into two equal pieces. The shaded half now contains two quarters.

Split every quarter in half again. Now four of eight equal pieces are shaded. One half, two quarters and four eighths are equivalent.

The number rule matches the picture. Multiply the numerator and denominator by the same non-zero number.

For three fifths, multiply both numbers by four. Three times four is twelve, and five times four is twenty.

Equivalent fractions also land at the same point on a number line. They are different names for one number.

Simplifying works in reverse. Group equal pieces together instead of splitting them into smaller pieces.

Group the eighths in pairs. Six eighths becomes three quarters. We divided the numerator and denominator by two.

Try twelve eighteenths. Both twelve and eighteen divide by six. Twelve divided by six is two; eighteen divided by six is three.

Be careful: adding the same number to both numbers does not preserve the value. One half and two thirds are different amounts.

We can also partition a fraction. Three quarters is three one-quarter pieces added together.

Two quarters make one half, so three quarters is also one half plus one quarter. Different partitions can describe the same amount.

Is six eighths equivalent to three quarters?

Yes. Dividing both six and eight by two gives three quarters.

Equivalent fractions name the same amount. Multiply or divide both numbers by the same non-zero factor. A model or number line helps you check that the value stays unchanged.
