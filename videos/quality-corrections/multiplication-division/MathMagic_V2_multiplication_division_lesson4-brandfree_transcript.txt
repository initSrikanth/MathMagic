If a division leaves something over, is the answer finished?

Today we are interpreting remainders.

We will compare a situation where everyone needs a place with a situation where the leftover amount can be shared.

The context decides what the remainder means.

Our learning goal is to interpret remainders to answer real-world division problems.

Success criteria.

I can check that a remainder is smaller than the divisor.

I can decide when a whole extra group is needed.

I can share a remainder as a fraction when appropriate.

A remainder must be smaller than the divisor.

Check a division using groups times group size plus the remainder.

Then decide whether to round up, keep whole groups, or share the remainder as a fraction.

In a small model, seventeen counters make three full groups of five, with two left over.

Sixty-seven students need teams of up to eight.

Eight full teams hold sixty-four students, leaving three students.

Everyone needs a team, so one more team is needed.

Nine teams have enough places, although the last team is not full.

Eight teams would leave three students out.

Share twenty-three litres equally among four containers.

Five litres in each container uses twenty litres, leaving three litres.

Share those three litres equally too.

Each container gets three quarters of a litre more, so the amount is five and three quarter litres, or five point seven five litres.

A remainder of three does not automatically mean point three.

In our litre problem, the three leftover litres are shared between four containers.

The divisor and the story explain the fractional part.

Thirty-one students travel in cars with five student seats each.

How many cars are needed?

Six cars hold thirty students, leaving one student.

A seventh car is needed, so the contextual answer is seven cars.
