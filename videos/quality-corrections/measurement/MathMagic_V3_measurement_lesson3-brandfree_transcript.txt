A school garden needs a fence.

Would counting the squares inside tell us how much fencing to buy?

Imagine walking all the way around its edge.

That journey measures the boundary, which is called the perimeter.

Our learning goal is to find the perimeter of regular and irregular shapes.

Success criteria.

I can trace the complete outside boundary.

I can use matching length units.

I can find missing lengths before adding sides.

Use string to trace a shape, then straighten the string beside a ruler.

This makes the idea of perimeter concrete.

On a diagram, follow every outside side once and add those lengths using matching units.

An inside dividing line is not part of the outside boundary.

A rectangular garden is seven metres long and four metres wide.

Opposite sides have matching lengths.

Trace the four edges and predict the total before we add them.

Seven plus four plus seven plus four is twenty-two metres.

We can also add seven and four, then double eleven.

Both methods count the complete boundary exactly once.

Now trace an L-shaped garden.

Its top is eight metres and its left side is six.

A three-by-two rectangle is missing from the lower right corner.

Use subtraction to find the remaining straight lengths.

The six outside edges measure eight, four, three, two, five and six metres.

Their sum is twenty-eight metres.

The inward corner still belongs to the outside boundary, so include both of its edges.

Seven times four counts square units covering this rectangle.

It does not measure the fence.

For perimeter, trace and add the outside lengths.

A useful check is whether your answer has metres or square metres.

A rectangle is six metres by three metres.

What is its perimeter?

Look at the model, and keep your prediction in mind.

Six plus three makes nine.

Double nine for the four sides: eighteen metres.

Check by tracing and adding six, three, six and three.

Perimeter is a length around the complete outside edge.

Trace before calculating, find hidden lengths and keep units consistent.

Try outlining a book with string, then compare a direct measurement with the total of its side lengths.

Reflect on which model helped you and why.
