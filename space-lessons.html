LESSON 1

3D objects and their properties

A museum needs labels for its collection of solids.

Learning goal

Identify a solid using its faces, edges and vertices.

Success criteria

  1. I can identify faces, edges and vertices
  2. I can distinguish flat faces from curved surfaces.
  3. I can justify how I recognise a solid.

Connect what you know

Think of a cereal carton and a die. Both have flat faces, but only the die has six equal square faces.

Explore with objects

Build a triangular prism with two card triangles and three rectangular strips. Mark each corner with a sticky dot; feel each join without counting it twice.

Connect the model to mathematical language

A cube has 6 square faces, 12 edges and 8 vertices. A triangular prism has 2 triangular and 3 rectangular faces, 9 edges and 6 vertices.

Worked example 1

A cube has 6 square faces. Count 4 edges around the top,4 around the bottom and 4 joining them:12 edges. Four top corners and 4 bottom corners give 8 vertices.

6 faces,12 edges,8 vertices

Worked example 2

A triangular prism has 2 triangular ends and 3 rectangular sides:5 faces. Each end has 3 corners, giving 6 vertices. Three edges on each end plus 3 joining edges gives 9 edges.

5 faces,9 edges,6 vertices

Predict and check

Common misconception

A cylinder has curved surface and two flat circular faces; do not call its circular boundaries vertices.

Try independently and explain why

Compare a pentagonal prism with a triangular prism. Explain how the number of side rectangles follows the number of sides on each end.

A helpful starting point

Use coloured stickers for vertices and a pencil mark on each counted edge. Turn the model to include hidden parts.

Take it further

For a pentagonal prism, justify 7 faces,15 edges and 10 vertices without relying on a memorised list.

Exit reflection

Explain one decision you checked today. What evidence would convince a partner, and which mistake would you now notice?

LESSON 2

Explore nets

A designer wants to make a gift box from one sheet of cardboard.

Learning goal

Predict and explain how a net folds to make an object.

Success criteria

  1. I can connect a 3D object to its 2D net
  2. I can predict which faces meet.
  3. I can explain why a proposed net works or fails.

Connect what you know

A flattened carton still contains the faces that made the box. Which joins did you cut, and which became folds?

Explore with objects

Cut six equal paper squares. Tape them edge to edge in the labelled cross shown. Predict the top, bottom and opposite faces before folding.

Connect the model to mathematical language

A cube net has six squares joined edge to edge, but not every arrangement of six squares folds into a cube. A square-based pyramid has a square base and four triangular side faces.

Worked example 1

In the labelled cross, A is opposite E, B is opposite D, and C is opposite F. Use C as a base, lift its neighbours, and track the remaining end face.

Opposite pairs:A–E, B–D, C–F

Worked example 2

A 2-by 3 block has 6 squares but will not make a cube. Folding forces repeated face directions, so some faces overlap. Counting squares is necessary but not enough.

6 squares does not guarantee a net

Predict and check

Common misconception

Six squares in a 2-by-3 rectangle overlap when folded into a cube: counting faces alone does not guarantee a valid net.

Try independently and explain why

Choose another arrangement in the net explorer. Predict its result, test, then explain a changed connection.

A helpful starting point

Label faces before folding and keep one face flat on the table.

Take it further

Find two cube nets that cannot be matched merely by rotating the paper. Explain how you know they are different.

LESSON 3

Design and test nets

A packaging company wants a carton that closes without overlapping faces.

Learning goal

Design, test and improve a net with matching face dimensions.

Success criteria

  1. I can sketch a net for a chosen prism
  2. I can check face count and dimensions.
  3. I can revise a net after predicting overlaps.

Connect what you know

A packaging designer cannot use six arbitrary rectangles: every closing edge must match its partner.

Explore with objects

Use squared paper to draw a 2-by 3-by 4 carton. Cut and fold with an adult. Tabs can attach faces, but tabs are not extra faces.

Connect the model to mathematical language

A rectangular prism with dimensions 2, 3 and 4 units needs two 2×3 faces, two 2×4 faces and two 3×4 faces. Matching edge lengths must meet when folded.

Worked example 1

Opposite faces come in pairs: two 2-by 3 rectangles, two 2-by 4 rectangles, and two 3-by 4 rectangles. This accounts for all 6 faces.

Three pairs of matching rectangles

Worked example 2

A square-based pyramid uses 1 square base and 4 triangular faces. The triangle bases match the four square edges; the triangle tips meet at one apex.

1 square+4 triangles=5 faces

Predict and check

Common misconception

A net is not correct just because it contains the right number of rectangles; the arrangement and adjoining edges matter.

Try independently and explain why

Explain why a cube net with one square stretched into a rectangle will fail to close as a cube.

A helpful starting point

Write matching edge lengths in the same colour, then compare every joined edge.

Take it further

Design two nets for the same carton. Explain which would waste less surrounding paper, without changing the box dimensions.

Exit reflection

Explain one decision you checked today. What evidence would convince a partner, and which mistake would you now notice?

LESSON 4

Coordinates and position

A rescue robot needs an exact location on a map.

Learning goal

Construct and read a grid using horizontal-first coordinates.

Success criteria

  1. I can identify the origin
  2. I can read horizontal then vertical coordinates.
  3. I can locate a point in the first quadrant.

Connect what you know

A grid-square name locates a region. A coordinate pair locates an exact intersection. Which is more precise for a robot?

Explore with objects

Lay string in equally spaced horizontal and vertical lines. Mark the origin where the zero lines meet. Use a counter to travel across first and up second.

Connect the model to mathematical language

Start at (0,0). The point (4,2) means travel four units horizontally to the right and two units up. Coordinates describe intersections, not grid squares.

Worked example 1

From(0,0), move 4 right and 2 up. The intersection is(4,2). A point at(2,4) is a different position because the movements are reversed.

(4,2):4 across,2 up

Worked example 2

Points(0,3) and(3,0) lie on different axes. A zero does not mean the point is missing: it means no movement along that direction.

Zero is a valid coordinate

Open the interactive model for this lesson

Predict and check

Common misconception

(2,4) and (4,2) are different points; the order matters.

Try independently and explain why

Plot(1,5),(5,1) and(3,3). Explain which has equal horizontal and vertical coordinates.

A helpful starting point

Say “across, then up” and trace one move at a time. Keep grid intervals equal.

Take it further

Construct your own first-quadrant grid and specify its unit spacing. Ask a partner to plot five points from your instructions.

Exit reflection

Explain one decision you checked today. What evidence would convince a partner, and which mistake would you now notice?

LESSON 5

Map routes and directions

A courier must visit three locations without getting lost.

Learning goal

Follow, construct and check a route using coordinates and directions.

Success criteria

  1. I can follow a sequence of coordinate moves
  2. I can write precise directions.
  3. I can check the final location.

Connect what you know

Giving a final destination is different from giving a route. The same place may be reached by different sequences of moves.

Explore with objects

Use a toy courier on a floor grid. Move it right, up and left, recording the position after every instruction.

Connect the model to mathematical language

Starting at (2,1), moving three units right and two units up ends at (5,3). Each move changes only the relevant coordinate.

Worked example 1

Start at(1,2). Right 4 reaches(5,2); up 3 reaches(5,5); left 2 reaches(3,5). The net movement is 2 right and 3 up.

Final position:(3,5)

Worked example 2

Start at(4,6). Down 2 reaches(4,4), then left 3 reaches(1,4). Check that the whole route remains within the map.

Final position:(1,4)

Open the interactive model for this lesson

Predict and check

Common misconception

A move of 'up 2' does not change the first coordinate.

Try independently and explain why

Find two different routes from(2,2) to(6,5). Describe which is shorter in grid units and justify any tie.

A helpful starting point

Record a small table with columns “move”, “across” and “up”.

Take it further

Plan a route through three landmarks while avoiding a blocked grid segment. Explain how you checked every step.

Exit reflection

Explain one decision you checked today. What evidence would convince a partner, and which mistake would you now notice?

LESSON 6

Translations

A game character slides across a tiled board.

Learning goal

Translate a shape while preserving every side length and angle.

Success criteria

  1. I can translate a shape
  2. I can describe the movement using directions and distances.
  3. I can identify properties that stay unchanged.

Connect what you know

A sticker slides across a page without spinning. Can one corner move a different distance and still count as the same slide?

Explore with objects

Trace a card triangle on transparent paper. Slide the paper without turning it. Join each original vertex to its image and compare the three movement arrows.

Connect the model to mathematical language

A translation of 3 right and 2 up moves every vertex by the same displacement. Side lengths, angles and orientation remain unchanged.

Worked example 1

Move(1,1),(3,1),(2,3) by 2 right and 1 up. The images are(3,2),(5,2),(4,4). Every vertex receives the same movement.

Add 2 across and 1 up to EVERY vertex

Worked example 2

A marker moves from(2,3) to(5,5). The translation is 3 right and 2 up. Applying this movement to(4,1) gives(7,3).

Movement:3 right,2 up

Open the interactive model for this lesson

Predict and check

Common misconception

Sliding a triangle is not rotating it; every point must move the same distance in the same direction.

Try independently and explain why

Describe a translation that takes one drawn triangle onto its partner. Verify it with all three vertices.

A helpful starting point

Use equal-length arrows pointing in the same direction from corresponding vertices.

Take it further

Show a supposed translation where one vertex moved differently. Explain why the result is distorted rather than translated.

Exit reflection

LESSON 7

Reflections

An artist wants a perfect mirror-image logo.

Learning goal

Reflect points and shapes using equal perpendicular distances.

Success criteria

  1. I can reflect a shape in a line
  2. I can identify corresponding points.
  3. I can explain equal perpendicular distances.

Connect what you know

Think of a reflection in a still pond. A point and its image are equally far from the mirror, on opposite sides.

Explore with objects

Fold paper along a straight line. Mark a point and press it through the fold to find its partner. Unfold and compare distances measured straight across the mirror.

Connect the model to mathematical language

For the vertical mirror line x=5, the point (3,2) reflects to (7,2). The original and image are each 2 units from the line.

Worked example 1

Reflect(3,2) in x=5. It is 2 units left of the mirror, so the image is 2 units right at(7,2). The mirror lies halfway between the pair.

(3,2) →(7,2) across x=5

Worked example 2

Reflect(4,1) in y=3. The point is 2 units below the line, so the image is 2 above it at(4,5). The horizontal coordinate stays 4.

(4,1) →(4,5) across y=3

Open the interactive model for this lesson

Predict and check

Common misconception

A reflected shape is not merely slid across the mirror. Match each point by equal perpendicular distances; a symmetrical shape may still look unchanged.

Try independently and explain why

Draw a triangle touching the mirror line. Explain why vertices on the line do not move.

A helpful starting point

Draw a short perpendicular connector from each vertex to the mirror before locating its partner.

Take it further

Create a reflected logo that looks different from a translated copy. Explain which feature demonstrates the reversal.

Exit reflection

Explain one decision you checked today. What evidence would convince a partner, and which mistake would you now notice?

LESSON 8

Rotations and symmetry

A windmill designer wants blades that match after a turn.

Learning goal

Rotate around a stated centre and investigate symmetry.

Success criteria

  1. I can rotate a shape around a specified centre
  2. I can recognise quarter and half turns.
  3. I can identify rotational symmetry.

Connect what you know

A windmill spins around its hub. Moving the hub changes where every blade travels, even if the turn angle stays the same.

Explore with objects

Trace a square on transparent paper. Pin the centre safely with adult help, then make quarter-turns. Count each position that matches the original outline.

Connect the model to mathematical language

A quarter-turn is 90°. A square matches itself after a 90° turn around its centre; a non-square rectangle first matches after 180°.

Worked example 1

A square matches after 90°,180°,270° and 360°, so its order is 4. The smallest positive matching turn is 90°. Count the full turn once, not zero and 360° as two separate positions.

Square:order 4

Worked example 2

A non-square rectangle first matches after 180° and again at 360°, giving order 2. An equilateral triangle has 3 matches; a shape with no smaller match has order 1.

Rectangle:order 2

Open the interactive model for this lesson

For reflection symmetry, a square has four lines: vertical, horizontal and the two diagonals. A non-square rectangle has just the vertical and horizontal midlines; its diagonals do not fold the entire shape onto itself. Test these lines in the explorer.

Predict and check

Common misconception

The centre of rotation matters; turning around a corner differs from turning around the centre.

Try independently and explain why

Use the symmetry explorer to compare a square, rectangle and scalene triangle. Test a possible mirror line as well as a rotation.

A helpful starting point

Mark the centre and keep a dashed original outline for comparison.

Take it further

Explain why a line passing through the centre is not automatically a line of symmetry. Find a counterexample.

Exit reflection

Explain one decision you checked today. What evidence would convince a partner, and which mistake would you now notice?

LESSON 9

Classification algorithms

Open the interactive shape-classification decision tree

A scientist needs a reliable way to sort shapes.

Learning goal

Create and test yes/no decisions that classify shapes by properties.

Success criteria

  1. I can choose observable properties
  2. I can build yes/no decisions.
  3. I can test and improve a classification algorithm.

Connect what you know

A computer cannot reliably answer “does it look like a square?” Which observable properties would make a reliable decision?

Explore with objects

Sort card shapes using yes/no questions. Put squares, non-square rectangles and non-square rhombuses in your collection so your rule faces genuine counterexamples.

Connect the model to mathematical language

Ask 'Does it have a curved surface?' to distinguish some solids. For polygons, ask 'Does it have exactly four sides?' then 'Are all four sides equal?' Each question must have a clear yes/no answer.

Worked example 1

A square has 4 equal sides AND 4 right angles. Equal sides alone can also describe a non-square rhombus. Right angles alone can describe a non-square rectangle.

Both conditions are needed

Worked example 2

A solid with 6 faces is not necessarily a cube. A non-cube rectangular prism has 6 faces too. Check whether all six faces are squares, with matching equal edges.

Test a rule using counterexamples

Open the interactive model for this lesson

Predict and check

Common misconception

'Does it look like a square?' is vague. A square is also a rectangle, so categories may overlap.

Try independently and explain why

Build a two-decision rule in the classifier. Explain which shapes still share a group and what further question would separate them.

A helpful starting point

Use a property table: dimension, side count, right angles, equal sides and curved surfaces.

Take it further

Repair a faulty rule without excluding valid boundary cases. Explain why every square is also a rectangle.

Exit reflection

LESSON 10

Integrated spatial design challenge

A park planner must build a model, map paths and design a repeating logo.

Learning goal

Combine a net, route and transformation in a justified design.

Success criteria

  1. I can select a valid net
  2. I can communicate coordinate routes.
  3. I can use transformations and justify classifications.

Connect what you know

A park planner needs a kiosk model, a delivery route and a mirrored logo. How can separate checks protect one complete plan?

Explore with objects

Use grid paper and card. Build a kiosk from a valid prism net, place its map point at an intersection, then trace and transform a sign nearby.

Connect the model to mathematical language

Create a small kiosk from a rectangular-prism net, plot its position on a first-quadrant map, translate a garden motif and classify its shapes with a branching algorithm.

Worked example 1

A courier starts at(2,2), moves 4 right and 3 up to(6,5), then 2 left to(4,5). Check each intermediate position before accepting the destination.

Check the route step by step

Worked example 2

A sign point at(2,3) moves 2 right to(4,3), then reflects in x=5 to(6,3). The slide must happen first; reversing the steps gives a different result.

Order of transformations matters

Open the interactive model for this lesson

Predict and check

Common misconception

A convincing diagram is not enough: coordinates, folding edges and transformations must all be mathematically consistent.

Try independently and explain why

Design a 10-by 10 park with 3 landmarks, a checked kiosk net and two transformed motifs. Label inputs, results and a reason for every decision.

A helpful starting point

Make a checklist for face connections, coordinate order, movement and classification properties.

Take it further

Swap designs with a partner and find a counterexample to one unchecked claim. Revise the design using evidence.

Exit reflection

Explain one decision you checked today. What evidence would convince a partner, and which mistake would you now notice?
