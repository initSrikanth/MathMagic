## Page 1 — Reading time and duration
**Learning goal:** Read analogue and digital times and calculate elapsed time accurately.
**Success criteria:** (1) Read times to the minute. (2) Convert between 12-hour and 24-hour notation. (3) Explain an elapsed-time calculation.
**Prior knowledge:** Count in fives around a clock and distinguish hour and minute hands.
**Concrete:** Move hands on a teaching clock from 9:35 am to 10:20 am. Count 25 minutes to 10:00, then 20 more minutes.
**Pictorial:** Draw an open number line: 9:35 → 10:00 (+25 min) → 10:20 (+20 min).
**Abstract:** 25 + 20 = 45 minutes. 14:05 means 2:05 pm.
**Worked example:** A library session starts at 11:48 am and ends at 12:32 pm. 12 min to noon plus 32 min = 44 min.
**Misconception:** 10:75 is not a valid clock time; 75 minutes after 10:00 is 11:15.
**Practice:** A bus leaves at 8:47 am and arrives at 9:26 am. Explain the 39-minute duration.
**Reflection:** Why is counting to the next hour often easier than subtracting clock numerals?

## Page 2 — Timetables and multi-step time problems
**Learning goal:** Interpret timetables and plan journeys using durations.
**Success criteria:** (1) Locate departure and arrival times. (2) Calculate waiting and travel times. (3) Justify a feasible schedule.
**Prior knowledge:** Find elapsed time across an hour boundary.
**Concrete:** Arrange departure, arrival and connection cards in chronological order.
**Pictorial:** Use a two-column timetable and a timeline showing travel and waiting segments.
**Abstract:** Total journey time = first journey + wait + second journey.
**Worked example:** Train A 08:15–08:52; connection 09:05–09:41. Travel 37 min, wait 13 min, travel 36 min; total 86 min.
**Misconception:** Total journey time includes waiting, not just time aboard transport.
**Practice:** A bus 10:28–11:03 connects to a train 11:18–11:54. Calculate the total journey time and waiting time.
**Reflection:** Which connection would you choose if you needed at least 10 minutes to change platforms?

## Page 3 — Understanding and estimating angles
**Learning goal:** Recognise an angle as an amount of turn and estimate its size.
**Success criteria:** (1) Identify the vertex and arms. (2) Classify acute, right, obtuse, straight and reflex angles. (3) Estimate angles using benchmark turns.
**Prior knowledge:** Quarter-turn, half-turn and full-turn.
**Concrete:** Rotate a hinged pair of rulers through quarter, half and three-quarter turns.
**Pictorial:** Draw rays from a common vertex and mark the smaller or larger turn explicitly.
**Abstract:** A full turn is 360°, a half turn 180°, a quarter turn 90°.
**Worked example:** A 135° angle is obtuse because it is greater than 90° and less than 180°.
**Misconception:** Longer arms do not create a larger angle; the amount of rotation does.
**Practice:** Classify 35°, 90°, 112°, 180° and 245°, then explain the benchmarks.
**Reflection:** How can you estimate an angle without a protractor?

## Page 4 — Measuring and constructing angles
**Learning goal:** Measure and draw angles using a protractor.
**Success criteria:** (1) Align the protractor centre with the vertex. (2) Read the correct scale from the starting ray. (3) Draw an angle to a specified measure.
**Prior knowledge:** Angles are measured in degrees.
**Concrete:** Place a protractor on a paper angle, aligning its baseline with one arm.
**Pictorial:** Show a 40° ray and a 140° ray on the same semicircular protractor, with their starting baselines highlighted.
**Abstract:** Read from 0° at the starting arm, not whichever printed scale is easier to see.
**Worked example:** To draw 65°, draw a baseline, centre the protractor at the endpoint, mark 65° on the correct scale, and join the endpoint to the mark.
**Misconception:** The two protractor scales are not interchangeable; choose the one whose zero aligns with the baseline.
**Practice:** Draw and label 30°, 75° and 125°. Estimate first, then measure.
**Reflection:** How could you detect a mistaken 115° reading for a 65° angle?

## Page 5 — Angles and time in real situations
**Learning goal:** Solve practical problems involving angle relationships and elapsed time.
**Success criteria:** (1) Select an appropriate representation. (2) Calculate an unknown angle or duration. (3) Check the result against a benchmark.
**Prior knowledge:** Straight angles total 180°, full turns total 360°, elapsed time can be split into intervals.
**Concrete:** Use a paper clock to model the minute hand moving from 12 to 3, then 3 to 8.
**Pictorial:** Mark the first quarter-turn as 90° and the next five hour-mark intervals as 150°.
**Abstract:** Twelve equal clock divisions represent 360° ÷ 12 = 30° each; a five-division turn is 150°.
**Worked example:** Two adjacent angles form a straight line. One measures 68°. The other is 180° − 68° = 112°.
**Misconception:** Clock-hand turns and the smaller angle between hands are not always the same; state which is being measured.
**Practice:** A rotating sign turns 120°, then 85° in the same direction. What further turn completes one revolution? 360° − 205° = 155°.
**Reflection:** Explain when a diagram, a timeline or an equation is the best tool for solving a problem.
