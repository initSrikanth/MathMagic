PAGE 1 • FRACTION SENSE

Understand, compare and order fractions

Key idea

A fraction describes equal parts of a whole. The numerator tells how many parts we have. The denominator tells how many equal parts make one whole.

For unit fractions, a larger denominator means smaller pieces.

Represent fractions in different ways

The same fraction can be shown as part of an area, a strip or a position on a number line. The whole must always be split into equal parts.

Area model

3 of 4 equal parts = 34

Strip model

Three quarter-lengths make 34.

Number line
01

34 is three equal jumps from 0.

Models help you see fraction size. They are especially useful when comparing fractions or deciding whether an answer is reasonable.

Worked example

34>24

Both denominators are 4, so compare the numerators: 3 parts are greater than 2 parts.

Put fractions in order

When denominators are related, rename fractions with equal-sized parts or place them on a number line. Then read them from smallest to largest.

14<12<34

PAGE 2 • EQUIVALENT FRACTIONS

Different names, same amount

Key rule

Equivalent fractions have the same value. Multiply or divide the numerator and denominator by the same number.

12=24=48

Partition a fraction

Partitioning means breaking a fraction into smaller fractions without changing its total value. A fraction can often be partitioned in more than one correct way.

34=14+14+14
34=12+14

The first example partitions 34 into unit fractions. The second uses equivalent fractions: 12 is the same as 24.

Worked example

Simplify:

1218÷ 6 →23

Divide both 12 and 18 by 6. The fraction is now in simplest form.

PAGE 3 • MIXED NUMBERS

Fractions greater than one

Key idea

An improper fraction has a numerator at least as large as its denominator. A mixed number combines a whole number and a fraction.

Worked example

114=234

11 ÷ 4 = 2 wholes with 3 quarters left over.

Compare mixed numbers

Compare the whole-number parts first. If the wholes are equal, compare the fractional parts using equivalent fractions, a model or a number line.

212>214

Both have 2 wholes, so compare 12 and 14. One half is greater.

Going the other way

325=175

3 × 5 + 2 = 17. Keep the denominator 5.

PAGE 4 • ADD & SUBTRACT

Add and subtract fractions

First look for the denominator relationship

Year 5 fraction problems often use related denominators. This means one denominator is a multiple of the other. Rename one fraction so both fractions have equal-sized parts.

Halves and quarters

One half is the same amount as two quarters. Rename halves as quarters when the other fraction is in quarters.

Thirds and sixths

One third is the same amount as two sixths. Rename thirds as sixths when the other fraction is in sixths.

Quarters and eighths

One quarter is the same amount as two eighths. Rename quarters as eighths when the other fraction is in eighths.

Ask: “Can I multiply the smaller denominator to make the larger denominator?” If yes, multiply that fraction's numerator by the same number.
12+14=24+14=34

Important: do not add the denominators. First rename the fractions so the parts are the same size.

Helpful extension: finding a least common multiple can also help you find a common denominator, but in this Year 5 topic the main strategy is to recognise related denominators.

Three strategies for addition and subtraction

Different representations can show the same calculation. Choose the one that helps you see the mathematics most clearly.

Area model

Shade parts of the same whole. This makes the size of each part visible.

Strip model

Line fraction strips up so equivalent parts and common denominators are easy to compare.

Number line

Start at the first fraction and make equal-sized jumps forward to add or backward to subtract.

Whichever strategy you use, the fractions must refer to the same whole and the parts must be the same size before their numerators can be combined.

Same denominator

When denominators are the same, add or subtract the numerators and keep the denominator.

27+37=57

Related denominators

12+14=24+14=34

Rename one half as two quarters, then add.

Mixed numbers with the same denominator

Keep the whole number, then add or subtract the fractional parts. If the fraction makes another whole, regroup it.

125+15=135

More than two fractions

12+14−18=48+28−18=58

Read the video transcript

How can we add a half and a quarter when their pieces are different sizes? Start with the same-sized whole.

First, try equal parts. Two sevenths plus three sevenths makes five sevenths. We count the parts. Their size stays the same, so the denominator stays seven.

For one half plus one quarter, rename the half. Split each half into two equal pieces. One half is two quarters.

Now add two quarters and one quarter. That makes three quarters. Do not add the denominators: we are still counting quarters.

Subtraction uses equal-sized parts too. Take three quarters minus one eighth. Each quarter is two eighths, so three quarters is six eighths.

Remove one eighth. Five eighths remain. On a number line, start at six eighths and jump back one eighth.

Sometimes we can simplify. One sixth plus three sixths is four sixths. Divide the numerator and denominator by two. Four sixths equals two thirds.

With mixed numbers, keep track of the wholes. One and four fifths plus two fifths is one and six fifths. Five fifths make another whole, so the answer is two and one fifth.

You can use more than two fractions. One half plus one quarter minus one eighth becomes four eighths plus two eighths minus one eighth. The answer is five eighths.

Your turn: two thirds minus one sixth. Pause and think. Two thirds is four sixths. Subtract one sixth to get three sixths, which is one half.

Remember: use the same whole, rename into equal-sized parts, calculate, then simplify if you can.

PAGE 5 • APPLY & REASON

Fractions in real situations

Plan before calculating

For a fraction problem, first identify the whole, then decide what each fraction represents. Ask whether you need to combine amounts, find a difference, find what remains, or compare two amounts.

Draw a strip, area model or number line when the relationship is hard to see. Estimate whether the answer should be less than 1, about 1, or greater than 1 before calculating.

Worked problem

Mia uses 12 cup of oats and 14 cup of nuts. How much altogether?

12+14=24+14=34
