PAGE 1 • FRACTION SENSE

Understand, compare and order fractions

Key idea

A fraction describes equal parts of a whole. The numerator tells how many parts we have. The denominator tells how many equal parts make one whole.

For unit fractions, a larger denominator means smaller pieces.

Represent fractions in different ways

The same fraction can be shown as part of an area, a strip or a position on a number line. The whole must always be split into equal parts.

Area model

3 of 4 equal parts = 34

Strip model

Three quarter-lengths make 34.

Number line
01

34 is three equal jumps from 0.

Models help you see fraction size. They are especially useful when comparing fractions or deciding whether an answer is reasonable.

Worked example

34>24

Both denominators are 4, so compare the numerators: 3 parts are greater than 2 parts.

Put fractions in order

When denominators are related, rename fractions with equal-sized parts or place them on a number line. Then read them from smallest to largest.

14<12<34

Which is bigger: three quarters or one half? Let’s build the amounts and find out.

A fraction starts with equal parts. Here, one whole is divided into four equal pieces.

Shade three pieces. The numerator, three, counts the shaded parts. The denominator, four, counts the equal parts in the whole.

A strip shows the same amount. Three of four equal lengths are shaded. The picture changes, but the value stays the same.

When denominators match, the pieces have the same size. Three quarters is greater than two quarters because three pieces are more than two.

Now compare one third and one sixth. These wholes are the same length, but the sixths are smaller pieces.

So one third is greater than one sixth. A bigger denominator does not always mean a bigger fraction.

Return to three quarters and one half. Split each half into two equal pieces. One half becomes two quarters.

Now the parts match. Three quarters is greater than two quarters, so three quarters is greater than one half.

A number line shows fraction size as distance from zero. Divide the distance from zero to one into four equal intervals.

One quarter is one interval from zero. One half is two intervals, and three quarters is three intervals.

Read from left to right to order them from smallest to largest: one quarter, one half, then three quarters.

Try this: which is greater, five eighths or three eighths? You can pause the video and explain your choice.

Five eighths is greater. Both use eighth-sized parts, and five parts are more than three. Compare equal wholes, use models, and check the number line.

PAGE 2 • EQUIVALENT FRACTIONS

Different names, same amount

Key rule

Equivalent fractions have the same value. Multiply or divide the numerator and denominator by the same number.

12=24=48

Partition a fraction

Partitioning means breaking a fraction into smaller fractions without changing its total value. A fraction can often be partitioned in more than one correct way.

34=14+14+14
34=12+14

The first example partitions 34 into unit fractions. The second uses equivalent fractions: 12 is the same as 24.

Worked example

Simplify:

1218÷ 6 →23

Divide both 12 and 18 by 6. The fraction is now in simplest form.

Can one half and four eighths be the same amount? Watch the shaded length as we change the pieces.

Start with one half. Split every part of the whole into two equal pieces. The shaded half now contains two quarters.

Split every quarter in half again. Now four of eight equal pieces are shaded. One half, two quarters and four eighths are equivalent.

The number rule matches the picture. Multiply the numerator and denominator by the same non-zero number.

For three fifths, multiply both numbers by four. Three times four is twelve, and five times four is twenty.

Equivalent fractions also land at the same point on a number line. They are different names for one number.

Simplifying works in reverse. Group equal pieces together instead of splitting them into smaller pieces.

Group the eighths in pairs. Six eighths becomes three quarters. We divided the numerator and denominator by two.

Try twelve eighteenths. Both twelve and eighteen divide by six. Twelve divided by six is two; eighteen divided by six is three.

Be careful: adding the same number to both numbers does not preserve the value. One half and two thirds are different amounts.

We can also partition a fraction. Three quarters is three one-quarter pieces added together.

Two quarters make one half, so three quarters is also one half plus one quarter. Different partitions can describe the same amount.

Your turn: three fifths equals how many twentieths? Pause if you want to work it out.

The answer is twelve twentieths. Multiply both numbers by four. Remember: change the name, keep the value.

Reason

If the denominator is multiplied by 4, what must happen to the numerator? Explain why.

PAGE 3 • MIXED NUMBERS

Fractions greater than one

Key idea

An improper fraction has a numerator at least as large as its denominator. A mixed number combines a whole number and a fraction.

Worked example

114=234

11 ÷ 4 = 2 wholes with 3 quarters left over.

Compare mixed numbers

Compare the whole-number parts first. If the wholes are equal, compare the fractional parts using equivalent fractions, a model or a number line.

212>214

Both have 2 wholes, so compare 12 and 14. One half is greater.

Going the other way

325=175

3 × 5 + 2 = 17. Keep the denominator 5.

What happens when we have more than one whole? Let’s count eleven quarter-sized pieces.

Four quarters make one whole. Another four quarters make a second whole. Three quarters are left over.

Eleven quarters is an improper fraction. Its numerator is at least as large as its denominator. It can describe one whole or more.

Two and three quarters is a mixed number. It combines two whole units with a fractional part.

To convert, divide eleven by four. Eleven divided by four is two with a remainder of three.

The denominator stays four because our pieces are still quarters. So eleven quarters equals two and three quarters.

Now go the other way. Take three and two fifths. Each whole contains five fifths.

Three times five gives fifteen fifths. Add the extra two fifths to make seventeen fifths.

So three and two fifths equals seventeen fifths. Multiply the whole number by the denominator, add the numerator, and keep the denominator.

A fraction can also equal an exact whole. Eight quarters is two wholes, with no fractional part left over.

To compare mixed numbers, compare the wholes first. Three and one quarter is greater than two and three quarters.

If the wholes match, compare the fractional parts. Two and one half is greater than two and one quarter.

Your turn: change nine quarters into a mixed number. How many groups of four can you make?

Nine quarters is two and one quarter. Two groups of four use eight quarters, leaving one quarter. Both forms name the same number.

PAGE 4 • ADD & SUBTRACT

Add and subtract fractions

First look for the denominator relationship

Year 5 fraction problems often use related denominators. This means one denominator is a multiple of the other. Rename one fraction so both fractions have equal-sized parts.

Halves and quarters

One half is the same amount as two quarters. Rename halves as quarters when the other fraction is in quarters.

Thirds and sixths

One third is the same amount as two sixths. Rename thirds as sixths when the other fraction is in sixths.

Quarters and eighths

One quarter is the same amount as two eighths. Rename quarters as eighths when the other fraction is in eighths.

Ask: “Can I multiply the smaller denominator to make the larger denominator?” If yes, multiply that fraction's numerator by the same number.
12+14=24+14=34

Important: do not add the denominators. First rename the fractions so the parts are the same size.

Helpful extension: finding a least common multiple can also help you find a common denominator, but in this Year 5 topic the main strategy is to recognise related denominators.

Three strategies for addition and subtraction

Different representations can show the same calculation. Choose the one that helps you see the mathematics most clearly.

Area model

Shade parts of the same whole. This makes the size of each part visible.

Strip model

Line fraction strips up so equivalent parts and common denominators are easy to compare.

Number line

Start at the first fraction and make equal-sized jumps forward to add or backward to subtract.

Whichever strategy you use, the fractions must refer to the same whole and the parts must be the same size before their numerators can be combined.

Same denominator

When denominators are the same, add or subtract the numerators and keep the denominator.

27+37=57

Related denominators

12+14=24+14=34

Rename one half as two quarters, then add.

Mixed numbers with the same denominator

Keep the whole number, then add or subtract the fractional parts. If the fraction makes another whole, regroup it.

125+15=135

More than two fractions

12+14−18=48+28−18=58

A half plus a quarter: do we get two sixths? No. We need equal-sized parts before we can combine them.

First, use matching denominators. Two sevenths plus three sevenths makes five sevenths.

We add the numerators and keep the denominator. We are still counting sevenths, not fourteenths.

For one half plus one quarter, the denominators are related: four is a multiple of two. Rename the half as two quarters.

Now add two quarters and one quarter. The result is three quarters.

Subtraction follows the same idea. For three quarters minus one eighth, rename three quarters as six eighths.

Take away one eighth. Five eighths remain. The denominator stays eight.

On a number line, begin at six eighths and move back one eighth. You land on five eighths.

Simplify when you can. One sixth plus three sixths is four sixths, which equals two thirds.

Mixed numbers can make another whole. One and four fifths plus two fifths is one and six fifths.

Regroup five fifths as one whole. The answer is two and one fifth.

For several fractions, rename them with a common denominator. One half plus one quarter minus one eighth becomes four eighths plus two eighths minus one eighth.

Four plus two minus one is five, so the result is five eighths.

Here is an extension: when neither denominator is a multiple of the other, shared multiples can help. Try one quarter plus one sixth.

Multiples of four include four, eight, twelve. Multiples of six include six, twelve, eighteen. Twelve is the smallest positive shared multiple.

One quarter is three twelfths. One sixth is two twelfths. Add to get five twelfths.

Your turn: two thirds minus one sixth. Rename two thirds as four sixths, then subtract one sixth.

Three sixths remain, which is one half. Use equal wholes, rename into equal parts, calculate, and simplify.

PAGE 5 • APPLY & REASON

Fractions in real situations

Plan before calculating

For a fraction problem, first identify the whole, then decide what each fraction represents. Ask whether you need to combine amounts, find a difference, find what remains, or compare two amounts.

Draw a strip, area model or number line when the relationship is hard to see. Estimate whether the answer should be less than 1, about 1, or greater than 1 before calculating.

Worked problem

Mia uses 12 cup of oats and 14 cup of nuts. How much altogether?

12+14=24+14=34

Mia mixes half a cup of oats with a quarter of a cup of nuts. How much does she use altogether?

First identify the whole: one cup. Altogether tells us to combine the amounts, so choose addition.

Rename one half as two quarters. Two quarters plus one quarter makes three quarters.

Mia uses three quarters of a cup altogether. That is more than half a cup and less than one cup, so the answer makes sense.

Now a ribbon is two metres long. We cut off three quarters of a metre. How much ribbon remains?

We want what remains, so subtract. Two metres equals eight quarter-metres.

Eight quarters minus three quarters is five quarters. Five quarters is one and one quarter.

One and one quarter metres remain. On the number line, move back three quarter-steps from two.

For a comparison, Ava walks three quarters of a kilometre. Leo walks five eighths of a kilometre. Who walks farther, and by how much?

Rename three quarters as six eighths. Ava walks farther because six eighths is greater than five eighths.

Subtract five eighths from six eighths. Ava walks one eighth of a kilometre farther.

Here is a two-step problem. A jug holds one litre. We pour out a quarter of a litre, then another half a litre. How much remains?

A quarter plus a half is three quarters of a litre poured out. Subtract three quarters from one whole litre.

One quarter of a litre remains. Check: three quarters poured out plus one quarter remaining makes one whole litre.
