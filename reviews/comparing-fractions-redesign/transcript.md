# Comparing Fractions — redesigned lesson

## Who ate more chocolate?

You and a friend each have a chocolate bar. Both bars are exactly the same size, and both are divided into four equal pieces. You eat three pieces. Your friend eats two. Who ate more chocolate? How could the picture help you explain your answer?

[Thinking time: 4 seconds.]

## Comparing fractions: explain why

You had one more of those equal-sized pieces. Today, we'll discover how to compare fractions, and explain why one amount is greater. We'll begin with pictures, then connect them to fraction numbers.

## Start with one whole

Let's build a fraction from the beginning. This rectangle represents one whole chocolate bar. When we divide a whole into parts, the parts must be equal in size. Unequal pieces wouldn't each be a quarter.

## Four equal parts

Here are four equal parts. Each part is one quarter of the whole.

## Choose three of those parts

Now choose three parts. The coloured region shows the amount we've chosen; the whole bar is still the same size.

## What does the four tell us?

We write that amount as three quarters. Look at the four below the fraction bar. What does that four tell us about the whole?

[Thinking time: 3 seconds.]

## Denominator: equal parts in the whole

Four tells us the whole is divided into four equal parts. We call the bottom number the denominator. It tells us the size of the parts we're using: these are quarters.

## And what does the three tell us?

Now look above the fraction bar. What does the three tell us?

[Thinking time: 3 seconds.]

## Numerator: parts chosen

It tells us that three parts are chosen. The top number is the numerator. Three chosen parts out of four equal parts make three quarters. The fraction and the picture describe the same amount.

## Same-sized pieces: make a prediction

Bring back your friend's bar. You chose three quarters; your friend chose two quarters. Both wholes are the same size, and both use quarter-sized pieces. Before we write a comparison, which coloured amount looks greater? Explain your prediction using the pieces.

[Thinking time: 4 seconds.]

## One extra quarter makes the difference

Look at the ends of the coloured regions. Both shares contain two quarters, but your share has one extra quarter. So three quarters is greater than two quarters. We know why: we're comparing different numbers of pieces that have exactly the same size.

## Now connect the picture to the symbol

Here's how we record that relationship. Three quarters is greater than two quarters. The wide opening of the comparison sign faces the greater amount. When denominators match, compare the numerators, because the equal parts already have the same size.

## One piece from each: which is larger?

What if the pieces aren't the same size? These two wholes are identical. Divide one into two equal parts and the other into four. If you take just one piece from each whole, which piece will be larger? Look at the size of each piece.

[Thinking time: 4 seconds.]

## Fewer equal parts means larger pieces

The piece from the first bar is larger. It's one half of the whole; the other piece is one quarter. Cutting the same whole into fewer equal parts makes each piece larger. We chose one piece each time, but those pieces have different sizes.

## A bigger denominator does not mean more

So one half is greater than one quarter. Notice something important: four is bigger than two, but one quarter is smaller than one half. A bigger denominator means more equal parts in the whole, not bigger pieces. With the same positive numerator, think about piece size.

## Different-sized parts: make a fair comparison

Now let's compare one half and two thirds. Start with the pictures. Which coloured region reaches further across its whole? Predict which fraction is greater, and think about how you could make the pieces the same size.

[Thinking time: 4 seconds.]

## Neither number alone tells the whole story

The two-thirds region reaches further. But comparing just the top numbers, or just the bottom numbers, isn't a reliable strategy when both are different. We need a fair way to name these amounts using equal-sized pieces. We can divide the existing pieces without changing what's coloured.

## Rename the half using sixths

Split each half into three equal pieces. The whole now contains six equal parts, and the coloured half covers three of them. We haven't added any chocolate. One half is the same amount as three sixths.

## Equivalent fractions: the same amount

These are equivalent fractions: different names for the same amount. We can record one half equals three sixths. The picture explains why multiplying both the numerator and denominator by three preserves the fraction's value.

## Rename two thirds using sixths

Now split each third into two equal pieces. Two coloured thirds become four coloured sixths. Again, the amount hasn't changed.

## Two thirds equals four sixths

Two thirds equals four sixths. We multiplied both the numerator and denominator by two, keeping the same amount.

## Equal-sized pieces make comparison clear

Now we can compare three sixths and four sixths. Four sixths is greater, because it contains one more sixth. So one half is less than two thirds. Six is a common denominator: it lets us compare the same-sized pieces. The new names made the comparison clearer.

## Your challenge: explain your choice

It's your turn. Which is greater: two thirds or three quarters? The wholes are equal in size. Use the models, and try to rename both fractions with equal-sized pieces. Think about twelfths. Take five seconds, or pause the video if you'd like more time.

[Thinking time: 5 seconds.]

## Turn thirds into twelfths

Split each third into four equal pieces. The two coloured thirds become eight twelfths. The shaded amount stays exactly the same.

## Record the first equivalent fraction

So two thirds equals eight twelfths.

## Turn quarters into twelfths

Split each quarter into three equal pieces. Three coloured quarters become nine twelfths. Again, only the divisions change, not the amount.

## Record the second equivalent fraction

Three quarters equals nine twelfths.

## Use equal parts to justify the answer

Compare eight twelfths with nine twelfths. Nine twelfths has one more twelfth, so three quarters is greater than two thirds. Did your prediction agree? The important part is explaining your answer using equal-sized pieces.

## What will you look for first?

Think back over the lesson. With the same denominator, compare how many equal parts are chosen. With the same numerator, compare the sizes of the pieces. With different denominators, use pictures or equivalent fractions to make a fair comparison. Always check that the wholes are equal in size. When you meet two new fractions, which strategy will you try first, and how will you explain why it works?
