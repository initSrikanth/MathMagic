# Comparing Fractions — shared narration

## Which would you choose?

Imagine two equal-sized chocolate bars. You can have three quarters of one bar, or one half of the other. Which gives you more chocolate? Keep your prediction in mind. We will use pictures to explain how to compare fractions, even when their numbers look quite different.

## Equal parts matter

First, check the wholes. Both bars must represent the same amount of chocolate. Otherwise, a small fraction of a giant bar could be larger than a big fraction of a tiny bar. Our models use equal-sized wholes, so the comparison is fair.

## Same denominator: compare the shaded parts

Look at three quarters and two quarters. The denominator is four in both fractions. It tells us that each whole is divided into four equal parts. Because the parts have the same size, we can compare how many are shaded.

## Three quarters is greater

Three quarter-sized pieces cover more of the bar than two quarter-sized pieces. So, three quarters is greater than two quarters. This comparison sign opens towards the larger fraction. With equal denominators, the larger numerator tells us which fraction is greater.

## Same numerator: think about piece size

Now compare one third with one sixth. Each fraction has a numerator of one, so we are choosing just one piece from each bar. But these pieces are different sizes. Look carefully at how the same whole has been divided.

## More parts means smaller pieces

Dividing the bar into six equal parts makes smaller pieces than dividing it into three equal parts. One third is therefore greater than one sixth. When positive fractions have the same numerator, the smaller denominator gives the greater fraction. The picture helps explain why.

## Different denominators: make equal-sized parts

Return to our chocolate choice: three quarters or one half. We cannot simply compare three with one, because quarters and halves are different-sized parts. Instead, let us describe both amounts using the same-sized pieces. Watch the half-bar model.

## One half is two quarters

Divide each half into two equal pieces. The whole now has four equal parts. The shaded half covers two of those quarters. Nothing has been added or taken away. One half and two quarters are equivalent fractions: different names for exactly the same amount.

## A common denominator makes comparison clear

We can also write this by multiplying the numerator and denominator of one half by two. That gives two quarters. Four is now a common denominator, so both fractions describe quarter-sized pieces. We changed the way we named the amount, not its value.

## Our chocolate question: solved

Now the comparison is clear. Three quarters is greater than two quarters, and two quarters equals one half. So three quarters is greater than one half. If the original bars are the same size, choosing three quarters gives you more chocolate. Does that match your prediction?

## Your turn: which fraction is greater?

Try one yourself. Which is greater: two thirds or five sixths? Think about how you could divide the thirds into smaller equal parts. Use the two models to help you. Take a few seconds to decide before the answer appears.

[Five seconds of thinking time.]

## Two thirds is four sixths

Each third can be split into two sixths. Two shaded thirds become four shaded sixths. So two thirds equals four sixths. Compare four sixths with five sixths: five sixths has one more sixth shaded, so it is greater.

## Three strategies to remember

Here are your three comparison strategies. With equal denominators, compare the numerators. With equal numerators, think about the size of the pieces. With different denominators, use equivalent fractions to make equal-sized parts. Always check that your wholes are equal. A clear picture can help you explain your answer.
