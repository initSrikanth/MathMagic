Reading time and duration

Learning goal

Read analogue and digital times and calculate elapsed time.

Success criteria

  1. Read time to the minute
  2. Convert 12-hour and 24-hour time
  3. Explain an elapsed-time calculation

Connect to what you know

You already know that an hour has 60 minutes and can read times to the nearest five minutes. Recall how to count forward across an hour.

Explore with objects

Move the hands on a classroom clock from 9:35 to 10:20.

Picture the idea

9:35 → 10:00 (+25 min) → 10:20 (+20 min).

Mathematical connection

25 + 20 = 45 minutes. 14:05 is 2:05 pm.

Worked example

A session from 11:48 to 12:32 lasts 12 + 32 = 44 minutes.

Watch out

Clock notation is base 60: 10:75 is 11:15, not a valid time display.

Compare time systems

12-hour time repeats 1–12 with am or pm. 24-hour time counts from 00:00 (midnight) to 23:59. For 1:00 pm to 11:59 pm, add 12 to the hour; for 13:00–23:59, subtract 12 to convert back to pm. Noon is 12:00 pm = 12:00; midnight is 12:00 am = 00:00.

Try it yourself

A bus leaves at 8:47 and arrives at 9:26. How long is the journey? Convert 19:45 into 12-hour time and explain your method.

Timetables and journeys

Practise reading the same time in 12-hour and 24-hour forms. The video and connection-planning activities also include optional next-year challenges. Connection planning is not part of your Year 5 quiz.

Read the transcript

A family is planning a trip to the wildlife park. Three buses leave at different times. Which one arrives before the gates open?

Today we are learning to read timetables and calculate travel durations.

Our learning goal is to read timetables and calculate travel durations.

Success criteria: read departure times, calculate elapsed time and choose a suitable journey.

Read the departure and arrival times from the same service on the timetable. Different rows can refer to different journeys.

A timeline makes the journey visible. Begin at eight twenty-five, move to nine o'clock and then move to nine ten.

How many minutes pass between eight twenty-five and nine ten? Estimate first, then calculate.

Thirty-five minutes to nine o'clock plus ten minutes to nine ten makes forty-five minutes.

Twenty-four-hour time removes confusion between morning and afternoon. Thirteen fifteen means one fifteen in the afternoon.

A train departs at thirteen fifteen and arrives at fourteen oh five. How long is the journey?

Forty-five minutes to fourteen hundred, then five more minutes. The journey lasts fifty minutes.

Subtracting the minute numbers alone is a mistake when you cross an hour. Use the full timeline instead.

For a deadline, consider arrival time, not just departure time. Arriving after an event begins may be too late.

You must arrive before ten. A service arrives at nine thirty-five. Does it meet the deadline?

Yes. Nine thirty-five is twenty-five minutes before ten, so the service meets the deadline.

Remember to include walking and waiting time. Reaching the station exactly when the bus leaves is risky.

A bus departs at nine twenty and arrives at ten oh five. How many minutes does the trip take?

Forty minutes to ten o'clock, then five more minutes. The trip lasts forty-five minutes.

Check that your answer is sensible. A journey crossing an hour can be longer than the difference between the minute digits.

A train leaves at eleven thirty-five and arrives at twelve twenty. Calculate the duration using noon.

Twenty-five minutes to noon plus twenty minutes afterwards makes forty-five minutes.

Read the correct timetable entry, draw a timeline, cross the hour boundary and add the intervals.

Think about a school bus timetable. The departure time tells us when to be at the stop, while the arrival time tells us when we can reach our destination.

If the bus is delayed, our original plan may no longer work. A good traveller checks whether there is enough time before an important appointment.

Try a different strategy. Instead of counting every minute, split the journey at the nearest whole hour and add the two intervals.

A journey from nine thirty-five to ten o'clock takes twenty-five minutes. We know this because sixty minus thirty-five equals twenty-five.

Check your answer by counting forward. Start at nine thirty-five, add twenty-five minutes, and you should reach ten o'clock.

If you have to change buses, calculate the waiting time between the first arrival and the second departure. That wait is part of the whole trip.

A timetable is a promise about planned times, but it is not the same as a clock. We use the timetable to predict when the journey should happen.

Suppose you need ten minutes to walk from the bus stop to the wildlife park. A bus arriving at nine fifty-five would not get you through the gate by ten.

When comparing two routes, the shortest ride is not always the earliest arrival. A later departure may still arrive sooner if it travels more directly.

Imagine a service that leaves at eight forty and arrives at nine twenty. Count twenty minutes to nine, then twenty more to nine twenty.

A transfer means leaving one service and boarding another. Calculate the gap between the first arrival and the second departure before choosing the route.

If your connection arrives at nine ten and the next bus leaves at nine twelve, you have only two minutes to change. Is that a sensible plan?

The answer depends on how far apart the platforms are. Good planning includes a safety margin, not only the arithmetic.

Now create a small timetable with two departure options. Give each an arrival time and decide which service is better for a morning appointment.

Estimate before you calculate. A journey from eleven thirty-five to twelve twenty must be shorter than an hour, because twelve thirty-five would be one hour later.

You can explain your thinking with words, numbers or a timeline. The strongest answer shows where each interval comes from.

Interpret a timetable and plan journeys involving connections.

Success criteria

  1. Read departure and arrival times
  2. Calculate waiting and travel times
  3. Justify a feasible connection

Connect to what you know

You can read a digital clock and find elapsed time. Recall that departure is when a service leaves and arrival is when it reaches its destination.

Explore with objects

Arrange departure, arrival and connection cards in chronological order.

Picture the idea

Example connection timetable

08:15 → 08:52 (37 min) → 09:05 (13 min wait) → 09:41 (36 min).

Check the connection: 09:05 is after 08:52, so there are 13 minutes to change services. If a bus arrived at 09:09, this train would already have departed.

Mathematical connection

Total journey = travel + wait + travel = 86 minutes.

Worked example

A bus from 10:28 to 11:03 lasts 35 minutes; a train at 11:18 adds 15 minutes waiting.

Watch out

Total journey duration includes waiting time, not only travel time.

Understanding angles

Read the transcript

Imagine a robot turning at a crossroads. It makes a quarter turn, then a half turn. How could we describe each turn accurately?

Today we are learning to recognise and compare angles as turns.

Our learning goal is to recognise and compare angles as turns.

Success criteria: identify vertices and arms, classify angles and compare degrees.

An angle is the amount of turning between two rays meeting at a vertex. The length of the rays does not change the angle.

A full turn is three hundred and sixty degrees, a half turn is one hundred and eighty, and a quarter turn is ninety.

Look at this angle. Is it acute, right, obtuse or straight? Pause for five seconds and decide.

Sixty degrees is smaller than ninety degrees, so this is an acute angle.

A right angle is ninety degrees. It looks like the corner of a square.

An angle is one hundred and twenty degrees. Is it acute or obtuse? Compare it with ninety degrees.

One hundred and twenty is greater than ninety and less than one hundred and eighty, so the angle is obtuse.

An angle with longer arms is not necessarily larger. We measure the amount of turning, not the length of the lines.

When measuring with a protractor, place the centre at the vertex and align one arm with the zero-degree baseline.

If the angle looks narrower than a right angle, should you read seventy-five or one hundred and five degrees?

Seventy-five degrees is the sensible measurement because it is less than ninety degrees.

Always check which protractor scale begins at zero along your starting arm. The two scales count in opposite directions.

Two adjacent angles lie on a straight line. One is forty degrees. What is the other angle?

A straight angle is one hundred and eighty degrees. Subtract forty to get one hundred and forty degrees.

To draw an angle, draw the first ray, mark the required number of degrees from zero and connect the mark to the vertex.

A robot makes two quarter turns in the same direction. How many degrees has it turned altogether?

Ninety plus ninety equals one hundred and eighty degrees, which is a half turn.

Angles are measured in degrees, not centimetres. A small drawing and a large drawing can show exactly the same turn.

Imagine a door opening. A small opening makes an acute angle, while a door opened square to the wall makes a right angle.

A turn of thirty degrees is one third of a right angle. Three such turns add up to ninety degrees.

A turn of one hundred and fifty degrees is close to a straight angle, but thirty degrees short of one hundred and eighty.

Use benchmarks to estimate. Compare the angle with ninety degrees before reading a protractor scale.

If your measurement says forty degrees but the drawing looks wider than a right angle, check whether you used the wrong scale.

When drawing an angle, keep the ruler steady and make sure both rays begin at the same vertex.

Explain your answer using the size of the turn. This is stronger than simply saying the angle looks big.

Imagine a compass needle pointing north. When it rotates to east, it has completed a quarter turn, even if the needle itself is very short.

If the compass turns from north to south, it has completed a half turn. The direction changed by one hundred and eighty degrees.

A turn of forty-five degrees is half of a right angle. We can compare it with the corner of a square to estimate its size.

An angle of eighty degrees is acute even though it is almost a right angle. The exact boundary is ninety degrees.

An angle of ninety-one degrees is obtuse, even though it looks very close to a right angle. Mathematical categories use precise boundaries.

Try drawing two rays with a narrow opening. Then rotate one ray without changing its length. Notice how the angle grows.

The vertex is the common endpoint. If the rays do not meet at the same point, we cannot identify the angle in the usual way.

Imagine a skateboarder changing direction by one hundred and thirty-five degrees. Explain why this turn is obtuse rather than straight.

Explain the steps to someone else: estimate the angle, align the baseline, read the correct scale and check the angle type.

Your challenge is to find three angles in your classroom, classify them and explain your reasoning.

Learning goal

Recognise an angle as an amount of turn and estimate its size.

Success criteria

  1. Identify vertex and arms
  2. Classify angle types
  3. Estimate with benchmark turns

Connect to what you know

You have met turns, corners and right angles in shapes. Recall that a quarter-turn makes a right angle.

Explore with objects

Rotate two rulers joined at a pivot through quarter, half and full turns.

Picture the idea

A quarter-turn is 90°, a half-turn is 180°, a full turn is 360°.

Mathematical connection

Acute: less than 90°. Right: 90°. Obtuse: 90°–180°. Straight: 180°. Reflex: 180°–360°.

Worked example

135° is obtuse because 90° < 135° < 180°.

Watch out

Longer arms do not make a larger angle; rotation does.

See and compare all six angle families

Angles describe the amount of turn between two arms meeting at a vertex. Arm length and diagram orientation do not change the angle. The open intervals below exclude their boundary values.

Explore the amount of turn

Estimate before measuring

Use 90° (quarter-turn), 180° (half-turn) and 360° (full turn) as benchmarks. A 120° turn is one right angle plus 30°; it is obtuse. Compare a 75° angle with 90°: it is 15° smaller and acute.

Try it yourself

Classify 35°, 90°, 112°, 180° and 245°. Sketch an angle in each family, and explain why changing the arm lengths does not change its size.

Reflect

How could you estimate 120° without a protractor?

Measuring angles

Read the transcript

Two students draw different-looking angles and both say theirs is bigger. How could we settle their argument fairly?

Before using a protractor, predict whether the angle is less than or greater than ninety degrees. Your prediction can catch a wrong-scale reading.

The centre mark must sit exactly on the vertex. If it is moved away, the protractor markings no longer measure the intended angle.

Some protractors number clockwise and anticlockwise at once. Find the zero aligned with the starting ray before following the numbers.

Suppose your first ray points left. Begin with the zero on the left, rather than automatically reading from the right-hand scale.

An angle of thirty degrees is much smaller than a right angle. Use this comparison when checking your drawing.

An angle of one hundred and sixty degrees is almost straight. It is twenty degrees away from a straight angle.

If you draw a sixty-degree angle and measure it again, your answer should be close to sixty degrees. Small pencil errors may matter.

For an independent check, draw an angle measuring one hundred and ten degrees. Predict its type before touching the protractor.

Learning goal

Measure and construct angles accurately using a protractor.

Success criteria

  1. Align centre and vertex
  2. Read the correct zero scale
  3. Draw a specified angle

Connect to what you know

You can identify acute, right and obtuse angles and estimate a quarter-turn. Recall that a protractor measures rotation in degrees.

Explore with objects

Place a protractor on a paper angle with its baseline aligned to one arm.

Picture the idea

Mark the starting ray at 0°, then find 65° on that scale.

Mathematical connection

To draw 65°, draw a baseline, align centre, mark 65° and join vertex to mark.

Worked example

A 40° angle is acute; a 140° angle is obtuse. Estimate before measuring.

Watch out

The inner and outer scales give different readings; always start at the correct zero.

Read a protractor accurately

Step 1: Place the centre mark on the vertex. Step 2: Align the 0° baseline with the first arm. Step 3: Follow the scale that begins at that 0°, not the opposite scale. Step 4: Check whether your reading agrees with the angle being acute or obtuse.

Construct an angle

Draw a ray with a ruler. Place the protractor centre on its endpoint, mark the required number of degrees on the scale starting at 0°, and draw the second ray through your mark. Measure again to check. For a reflex angle, construct or measure the smaller angle and subtract it from 360°.

Interactive angle construction studio

Construct a requested angle by dragging the blue endpoint or using the keyboard-accessible slider. First estimate its size using 90° and 180° benchmarks. Then check your construction. The target stays hidden until you check.

Construct an angle of 35°.

Drag the blue handle, use the slider or press arrow keys. Each arrow-key step changes the angle by 1°.

Challenge: explain how you know whether the angle you made is acute, obtuse or reflex. Use a real protractor to construct the same angle on paper.

Try it yourself

Estimate, draw and measure 30°, 75° and 125° angles with a physical protractor. Compare your estimate with your measured result and explain any difference.

Learning goal

Apply time and angle relationships in practical situations.

Success criteria

  1. Select a suitable model
  2. Convert time displays in practical problems
  3. Check against benchmarks

Connect to what you know

You can calculate durations, read timetables and estimate angle sizes. Recall that a full turn is 360° and a straight angle is 180°.

Explore with objects

Rotate a clock hand across 5 equal hour-mark intervals.

Picture the idea

12 divisions in 360° gives 30° per division; 5 divisions gives 150°.

Optional next-year puzzle

The following angle relationships are extra exploration. They are not part of your Year 5 challenge.

Angles on a straight line total 180°. Angles around a point total 360°.

Extra worked example

If an angle on a straight line is 68°, its partner is 180° − 68° = 112°.

Watch out

A clockwise turn is not always the smaller angle between two clock hands.

Extra puzzle

A sign turns 120° then 85°. How much farther to complete 360°?

Use two time systems

An activity starts at 11:35 am and lasts 50 minutes. Change the start to 11:35 on the hall’s 24-hour clock. Count 25 minutes to noon, then 25 more: the finish display is 12:25. A ticket for 2:05 pm matches 14:05, not 02:05. Add 12 only to the hour; the minutes stay the same.
