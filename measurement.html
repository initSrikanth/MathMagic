Choose Units and Measure Length

Today’s Learning Goal

Measure length accurately using useful metric units.

Success Criteria: I can…

  • I can choose a sensible unit and instrument.
  • I can read a ruler from the correct starting point.
  • I can combine metres and centimetres accurately.

Investigate first

Could the same pencil seem longer just because someone moved the ruler? Imagine two friends measuring it from different marks. Before we trust a number, let us investigate the unit, the scale and the starting point.

Remember that a measure counts equal units. Millimetres help with small details, centimetres suit a pencil, metres suit a room, and kilometres suit a long journey. Choose a tool with useful markings. Smaller units can give a more precise measurement, rather than changing the object.

Guided example 1

A ribbon begins at the two centimetre mark and ends at nine centimetres. Predict its length. Count the equal one-centimetre intervals between those marks, rather than reading the final label alone.

9 − 2 = 7 cm
Length is the difference

There are seven centimetres between the marks. Nine minus two equals seven. Moving the ruler does not stretch the ribbon: subtract the starting reading whenever the object is not lined up with zero.

Guided example 2

A display board measures two metres and thirty-five centimetres. We want a single centimetre reading. One metre contains one hundred centimetres, so the two metres account for two hundred centimetres.

200 + 35 = 235 cm
2 m 35 cm = 235 cm

Add the remaining thirty-five centimetres. Two hundred plus thirty-five makes two hundred and thirty-five centimetres. The combined measure and the single-unit measure describe exactly the same length.

Check a common mistake

Reading nine centimetres as the ribbon length ignores where it started. Count the space between the marks. A different starting mark changes the subtraction, but not the physical ribbon.

Think, then check

A strip starts at three centimetres and ends at eleven centimetres. How long is it?

Practical challenge and reflection

Choose a useful unit, read the interval size and check the starting mark. For a practical investigation, measure a pencil in centimetres and millimetres. Discuss which reading describes its length more precisely. Reflect on which model helped you and why. Now try the MathMagic practice, and explain your thinking to someone.

Measure Mass and Capacity

Today’s Learning Goal

Measure mass and capacity using scales and combined units.

Success Criteria: I can…

  • I can distinguish mass from capacity.
  • I can read equal scale intervals.
  • I can combine kilograms and grams or litres and millilitres.

Investigate first

A lunchbox feels heavy, while a bottle looks full. Are we measuring the same attribute? Imagine packing for a picnic. We need a balance for mass and a marked container for capacity. Let us choose useful units before measuring.

Mass describes how heavy an object is; capacity describes how much a container can hold. Use grams or kilograms for mass, and millilitres or litres for capacity. One kilogram is one thousand grams. One litre is one thousand millilitres. On every scale, count equal intervals before deciding what one interval represents.

Guided example 1

A bag has a mass of one kilogram and three hundred and fifty grams. Picture one thousand grams as a whole kilogram, with the smaller amount added alongside. What single gram reading would describe the same mass?

1,000 + 350 = 1,350 g
1 kg 350 g = 1,350 g

One thousand plus three hundred and fifty is one thousand three hundred and fifty grams. The bag has not become heavier because we changed its name. Kilograms and grams are two units for the same attribute.

Guided example 2

A jug holds two litres and two hundred and fifty millilitres. Convert the whole litres first. Two groups of one thousand millilitres make two thousand millilitres. Then include the extra marked amount.

2,000 + 250 = 2,250 mL
4 equal intervals: 250 mL each

The capacity is two thousand two hundred and fifty millilitres. On a scale from zero to one thousand with four equal intervals, each interval would be two hundred and fifty millilitres. Count intervals, not just tick marks.

Check a common mistake

Grams and millilitres are not interchangeable labels. A balance measures mass; a marked jug measures capacity. Equal numerical readings do not make those attributes the same. Read the instrument and include the correct unit.

Think, then check

A balance points halfway between five hundred and six hundred grams. What mass does it show?

Practical challenge and reflection

Choose the attribute, unit and instrument together. For a practical challenge, use a kitchen balance or marked jug with an adult. Predict a reading, measure carefully, and explain the difference between your estimate and observation. Reflect on which model helped you and why. Now try the MathMagic practice, and explain your thinking to someone.

Trace and Calculate Perimeter

Today’s Learning Goal

Find the perimeter of regular and irregular shapes.

Success Criteria: I can…

  • I can trace the complete outside boundary.
  • I can use matching length units.
  • I can find missing lengths before adding sides.

Investigate first

A school garden needs a fence. Would counting the squares inside tell us how much fencing to buy? Imagine walking all the way around its edge. That journey measures the boundary, which is called the perimeter.

Use string to trace a shape, then straighten the string beside a ruler. This makes the idea of perimeter concrete. On a diagram, follow every outside side once and add those lengths using matching units. An inside dividing line is not part of the outside boundary.

Guided example 1

A rectangular garden is seven metres long and four metres wide. Opposite sides have matching lengths. Trace the four edges and predict the total before we add them.

7 + 4 + 7 + 4 = 22 m
2 × (7 + 4) = 22 m

Seven plus four plus seven plus four is twenty-two metres. We can also add seven and four, then double eleven. Both methods count the complete boundary exactly once.

Guided example 2

Now trace an L-shaped garden. Its top is eight metres and its left side is six. A three-by-two rectangle is missing from the lower right corner. Use subtraction to find the remaining straight lengths.

8 + 4 + 3 + 2 + 5 + 6 = 28 m

The six outside edges measure eight, four, three, two, five and six metres. Their sum is twenty-eight metres. The inward corner still belongs to the outside boundary, so include both of its edges.

Check a common mistake

Seven times four counts square units covering this rectangle. It does not measure the fence. For perimeter, trace and add the outside lengths. A useful check is whether your answer has metres or square metres.

Think, then check

A rectangle is six metres by three metres. What is its perimeter?

Practical challenge and reflection

Perimeter is a length around the complete outside edge. Trace before calculating, find hidden lengths and keep units consistent. Try outlining a book with string, then compare a direct measurement with the total of its side lengths. Reflect on which model helped you and why. Now try the MathMagic practice, and explain your thinking to someone.

Equal sides and regular shapes

A square has four equal sides: four 5 cm sides give 4 × 5 = 20 cm. A regular hexagon has six equal sides and equal angles: six 4 cm sides give 6 × 4 = 24 cm. Trace all sides once; equal side lengths let you multiply instead of adding repeatedly.

Cover and Calculate Area

Today’s Learning Goal

Explain and find area using square units.

Success Criteria: I can…

  • I can cover a surface without gaps or overlaps.
  • I can use rows and columns to count squares.
  • I can distinguish area from perimeter.

Investigate first

How many equal square tiles would cover a small floor? A long piece of string cannot cover the surface. We need a different measure from perimeter: area tells us how much flat space a shape covers.

Arrange equal unit squares without gaps or overlaps. Each square covers one square unit. For a rectangle, equal rows let us count efficiently with multiplication. A square centimetre is a square one centimetre along each side, not a one-centimetre length.

Guided example 1

A rectangle has eight columns and five rows of square-centimetre tiles. Predict how many unit squares cover it. Each row contains eight squares, and there are five equal rows.

8 × 5 = 40 cm²

Five groups of eight make forty unit squares. The area is forty square centimetres. Multiplying eight centimetres by five centimetres counts the same tiled surface, so we write the squared unit.

Guided example 2

A play mat is seven metres by four metres. Imagine covering it with one-metre squares. There are seven squares along each row, with four rows altogether. What would the surface measurement be?

Area: 7 × 4 = 28 m²
Perimeter: 7 + 4 + 7 + 4 = 22 m

The area is twenty-eight square metres. The perimeter is a different quantity: seven plus four plus seven plus four is twenty-two metres. Area counts the cover; perimeter measures the border.

Check a common mistake

Adding eight and five does not count the tiles covering an eight-by-five rectangle. It counts only two side lengths. To find area, use all five rows of eight equal squares, with no gaps or overlaps.

Think, then check

A rectangle has six columns and four rows of unit squares. What is its area?

Practical challenge and reflection

Area counts equal squares covering a surface. Use rows and columns to count efficiently and always label the square unit. Draw two rectangles with the same area on squared paper. Do they have to have the same perimeter? Explain with a drawing. Reflect on which model helped you and why. Now try the MathMagic practice, and explain your thinking to someone.

Design with Irregular Shapes

Today’s Learning Goal

Solve practical problems with irregular shapes.

Success Criteria: I can…

  • I can split a shape or subtract a missing rectangle.
  • I can check every outside length for perimeter.
  • I can compare designs using the required measure.

Investigate first

A playground has a corner cut out for a tree. Can we still work out how many paving tiles it needs? An irregular outline does not stop us measuring. Let us turn the tricky shape into familiar rectangles.

Build an L shape with square tiles, then slide a pencil along possible dividing lines. You can split its area into non-overlapping rectangles, or start with a large rectangle and subtract the missing part. Both methods must cover exactly the same surface.

Guided example 1

An L-shaped space fits inside an eight-metre by six-metre rectangle. A three-metre by two-metre corner is missing. Predict whether its area is greater or smaller than forty-eight square metres.

48 − 6 = 42 m²
8 × 4 + 5 × 2 = 42 m²

Subtract the missing six square metres from forty-eight. The L-shaped area is forty-two square metres. As a check, split it into an eight-by-four rectangle and a five-by-two rectangle. Thirty-two plus ten also gives forty-two.

Guided example 2

A larger design fits inside a ten-metre by eight-metre rectangle, with a four-by-three corner missing. Plan the area calculation first. Keep the border measurement separate from the surface measurement.

Area: 80 − 12 = 68 m²
Border: 10 + 5 + 4 + 3 + 6 + 8 = 36 m

The usable area is sixty-eight square metres. For its border, trace ten, five, four, three, six and eight metres. That perimeter is thirty-six metres. Different questions about the same design need different measures.

Check a common mistake

Using the full eight-by-six rectangle would include the tree corner. That overcounts six square metres. Subtract the missing rectangle, or add two rectangles that cover only the actual playground.

Think, then check

A six-by-five rectangle has a two-by-two corner removed. What area remains?

Practical challenge and reflection

Choose the measure the problem needs. For area, split or subtract rectangles; for perimeter, trace every outside edge. Draw a garden on squared paper and explain a paving plan and a fencing plan. Check that your answers describe the actual design. Reflect on which model helped you and why. Now try the MathMagic practice, and explain your thinking to someone.

Compare plans and buy whole packs

Garden A is 8 m by 3 m: border 22 m, area 24 m². Garden B is 7 m by 4 m: border 22 m, area 28 m². The same border length can surround different areas.

For a 6 m by 5 m space with a 2 m by 2 m corner missing, the usable area is 30 − 4 = 26 m². One tile covers 1 m². Packs contain 6 tiles: four packs cover 24 m², so buy 5 whole packs to cover all 26 m². Explain the remainder in the real situation.

If an L shape covers 42 m² inside an 8 m by 6 m rectangle, its missing area is 48 − 42 = 6 m². A missing corner 3 m wide must be 6 ÷ 3 = 2 m high.

Check a complete design decision

A garden 8 m by 3 m has area 24 m² and border 22 m. A 7 m by 4 m garden has area 28 m² and the same 22 m border. At $3 per metre of fencing and $2 per square metre of paving, totals are $66 + $48 = $114 and $66 + $56 = $122. The smaller-area plan is cheaper, even though border costs match.

Six packs of four 1 m² tiles cover 24 m². If all tiles cover a floor 6 m wide with no leftovers, its length is 24 ÷ 6 = 4 m. Decide whether the problem asks for a length, area, quantity or cost before calculating.
