Our learning goal is to describe turns and test rotational symmetry. We want to explain why a result is correct, as well as obtain the result. As you watch, look for the evidence that would help you convince a partner who has not seen your working.

Here are our three success criteria. I can name the centre and turn. I can track corresponding points. I can count matching positions. We will check these ideas in two worked examples, investigate a common mistake and finish with an independent challenge. A good explanation links what you see in the model with the mathematical rule being used.

Trace a simple shape, mark a centre and hold that point still with a pencil tip. Turn the tracing around the centre without sliding it. Every point follows a circular path around the same fixed position. The original outline remains as a reference for comparing the new position.

A useful rotation instruction names the centre, the size of the turn and its direction. A quarter turn is ninety degrees, a half turn is one hundred and eighty degrees, and a full turn is three hundred and sixty degrees. Clockwise follows the direction of clock hands.

During a rotation, each point keeps its own distance from the centre. Different points may have different distances, but every point turns through the same angle. This distinguishes a turn from moving corners independently. Side lengths and angles of the whole shape remain unchanged.

The centre is four, four. Point A is at six, four, directly two units to its right. Predict where A lands after a quarter turn anticlockwise. Imagine the radius turning from pointing right to pointing up, while keeping the same two-unit length.

The radius turns upward, so A lands at four, six. It remains two units from the centre. The centre itself stays at four, four. Turning the whole shape means using that same quarter turn for every corner, rather than treating A as the new centre for the next point.

Starting again from six, four, a clockwise quarter turn points the radius downward. The image is four, two. Both images are the same distance from the centre, but their positions differ because the turn directions differ. Always include direction when a quarter turn could be interpreted either way.

A student turns the tracing around one corner after being given the centre of the grid. The new shape may still have the same side lengths, but it is not the requested rotation. Put the pencil back on the named centre and repeat the turn. Location matters as well as angle.

A shape has a matching position when turning it around its centre makes it cover its original outline exactly. We count distinct matches during one full turn, including the final full-turn match. Our convention calls a shape with only that final match order one.

Imagine turning an unmarked square around its centre. At which quarter-turn stages will the outline cover the original? Count the matching positions during one full turn, but do not count the starting position as an extra match. Prepare to explain each position rather than guess from the number of sides.

The square matches after ninety, one hundred and eighty, two hundred and seventy, and three hundred and sixty degrees. That gives rotational symmetry of order four. We count the full-turn match once. Counting both zero degrees and the full turn as separate end positions would incorrectly give five.

A non-square rectangle fails to match after a quarter turn because its long direction has become its short direction. It matches after a half turn and after a full turn. There are two matching positions in one full turn, so its rotational symmetry has order two.

A reflection uses a mirror line and reverses orientation. A rotation uses a centre and a turn direction. A shape may have both kinds of symmetry, but a line of reflection is not a centre of rotation. Name the transformation before explaining what stays the same.

An ordinary uneven shape may only match after one full turn. Under our convention that is order one. Do not assume every shape has a useful smaller matching turn. Tracing the outline and testing a complete revolution gives evidence, while looking at one corner usually does not.

A student says a non-square rectangle has order four because it has four sides. Test that claim with a quarter turn. Will a long horizontal edge cover a short vertical edge? Decide which matching turns are actually supported by the whole outline.

The rectangle does not match after a quarter turn, so four sides do not prove order four. The half-turn and full-turn positions do match, giving order two. The order is determined by exact covering positions around a centre, not simply by a count of sides or corners.
