Here are our three success criteria. I can locate the mirror line. I can match equal perpendicular distances. I can check the reflected shape. We will check these ideas in two worked examples, investigate a common mistake and finish with an independent challenge. A good explanation links what you see in the model with the mathematical rule being used.

Draw a simple shape on paper, fold the page along a straight line and imagine transferring the marks to the other half. When opened, matching points lie on opposite sides of the fold. The fold gives the mirror line, which controls the position of every reflected point.

Our first mirror is vertical, passing through horizontal coordinate five. We can describe it as the line x equals five. Every point on that line has first coordinate five. The mirror is a whole line, not a single point to rotate around or a boundary that moves with the shape.

For a vertical mirror, measure horizontally from the point to the line. That direction is perpendicular to the mirror. Place the image the same distance on the opposite side. The height stays unchanged. Measuring diagonally would give the wrong matching position even if the diagonal lengths happened to agree.

Point A is at two, three. The mirror is the vertical line through five. How many units separate A from the mirror? Predict the image coordinates by placing its partner the same distance on the other side, at the same height. Do not simply swap the coordinate numbers.

A is three units left of the mirror. Its image must be three units right of the mirror, at eight, three. The two points share the same height. Their joining segment crosses the mirror at a right angle, and the mirror cuts that segment into two equal lengths.

Our triangle also has B at four, three and C at two, five. B lies one unit left of the mirror, so its image is six, three. C lies three units left, so its image is eight, five. Join the reflected corners in matching order to complete the image.

A and B do not travel the same horizontal distance between original and image. A crosses six units, while B crosses two. Their distances depend on how far they start from the mirror. Applying one shared slide would be a translation, and would not produce this reflected triangle.

A point on the mirror line is zero units away from it. Its reflected position is therefore the same point. This is a useful check when a shape touches or crosses the mirror. A reflection does not force every point to move to a different location.

Now use a horizontal mirror at height four. Point A is at two, one. Predict its image by measuring straight upward to the mirror and the same distance beyond it. Which coordinate should stay unchanged this time, and which one must change?

The point is three units below the mirror, so its image is three units above, at two, seven. The first coordinate stays two. The vertical distances to height four are equal. The same distance rule works for either orientation of mirror; only the perpendicular direction changes.

A reflection preserves side lengths and angles, so the image is the same size and shape. Its orientation is reversed, like writing seen in a mirror. Compare matching corners rather than deciding only from the outline. Symmetric shapes can conceal the reversal, making point checks especially useful.

Reflect the image across the same mirror a second time. Each point returns to its original location because the same equal distance rule acts in reverse. This provides a practical way to check your construction. If a point does not return, inspect the mirror position and its distance first.

When comparing the original and image, keep the mirror line in the same place. Moving the line halfway through creates a different transformation. Label the line clearly and draw the matching point pairs. That makes your reasoning visible to someone who did not watch you construct the image.

A point at four, three reflects in the vertical line through five. A student puts its image at seven, three. Is the new point the correct distance from the mirror? Compare the original and proposed distances before deciding where the image really belongs.

The original point is one unit left of the mirror. Seven, three is two units right, so the distances do not match. The correct image is six, three, one unit right. A clear distance check explains exactly why the first proposal failed and why the repair works.
