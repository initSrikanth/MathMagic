Here are our three success criteria. I can find the origin and axes. I can read horizontal first. I can plot points at intersections. A good explanation links what you see in the model with the mathematical rule being used.

Draw horizontal and vertical lines at equal intervals on squared paper. Use a ruler if it helps. The lines meet at intersections. Mark a starting point in the lower left, then number the horizontal and vertical directions from zero. Both sets of numbers need a clear, consistent scale.

The point where the two numbered axes meet is the origin. Its coordinates are zero, zero. Zero means no movement from the starting point in that direction. The origin is a real point on the grid, not an empty space between the first two labelled lines.

An ordered pair gives the horizontal coordinate first and the vertical coordinate second. We write the pair in brackets with a comma between the numbers. The order matters because each position in the pair has a different job. Today every point stays on this first-quadrant map or its boundary.

Our first landmark has coordinates two, three. Start at the origin. Which direction should you travel for the two, and which direction for the three? Imagine tracing the journey with your finger. Keep the destination in mind while we apply the horizontal-first convention.

Travel two units right from the origin, then three units upward. Mark the intersection reached by those movements. The point is two, three. The path helps us locate it, but the ordered pair names the destination, not every step of the path used to arrive there.

To read the same point, look straight down to find its horizontal coordinate. Then look straight across to find its vertical coordinate. Record those numbers in that order. We can check our reading by plotting the pair again and seeing whether it returns to the original intersection.

A coordinate point sits at the crossing of two grid lines. It does not refer to the centre of a square cell. Count intervals between grid lines rather than counting painted squares at random. A clear dot at the intersection removes doubt about the location being described.

Now compare two, three with three, two. The first point is two across and three up. The second is three across and two up. Both numbers appear in both pairs, yet the points differ. Swapping numbers usually changes a location because it changes the job of each number.

A flag is five units across and one unit above the horizontal axis. Predict its ordered pair before the label appears. Ask yourself which number should be written first. You can check your choice by describing the journey from the origin aloud.

The flag is at five, one. Five gives the horizontal position and one gives the vertical position. To check, return to the origin, move five right and one up. The check returns us to the flag, so the words, numbers and drawing agree.

A point at zero, four needs no horizontal movement and four units upward. It lies on the vertical axis. A point at four, zero lies on the horizontal axis instead. A zero coordinate is useful information, so do not omit it when writing either pair.

Each gap on our grid represents one unit. On another map, a gap could represent two units or ten metres. Read the labels before interpreting a position. Counting three gaps does not automatically mean the coordinate is three unless the stated scale makes each gap one unit.

A helpful instruction gives a named point and its complete ordered pair. Saying that the treasure is near the top is too vague for another explorer. Saying that it is at two, three lets someone place the same point without seeing your original map.

A student labels a point four, zero but draws it on the vertical axis above the origin. Decide what went wrong. Which direction does the first number describe, and what does the zero require? Explain the correction rather than merely replacing the dot.

The point four, zero belongs four units right along the horizontal axis. Its second coordinate requires no upward movement. The mistake was exchanging the jobs of the two coordinates. Always check both the order and the scale, especially when a point lies on an axis.
