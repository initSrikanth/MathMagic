Our learning goal is to follow and describe routes using coordinates and direction. We want to explain why a result is correct, as well as obtain the result. As you watch, look for the evidence that would help you convince a partner who has not seen your working.

Here are our three success criteria. I can track each route step. I can update the correct coordinate. I can check a destination and distance. We will check these ideas in two worked examples, investigate a common mistake and finish with an independent challenge. A good explanation links what you see in the model with the mathematical rule being used.

Put a counter at an intersection on squared paper. Move it along grid lines, one interval at a time. Say right, left, up or down as you move. Record a new ordered pair after each part of the journey, instead of waiting until the very end.

A move right increases the first coordinate. A move left decreases it. The second coordinate stays unchanged because the counter has not moved up or down. Point to the row before and after moving. Both positions lie on the same horizontal line of the map.

A move upward increases the second coordinate. A move downward decreases it. The first coordinate stays unchanged. Check the column containing both points to see why. These rules describe our map directions; a rotated map would need its directions clarified before someone followed your instructions.

Start at two, two. Move four units right and then three units up. Predict the destination. Keep an intermediate position for the end of the rightward move. That extra record will help you discover which step needs fixing if your answer differs from your partner’s.

Moving four right changes two, two to six, two. Moving three up then changes six, two to six, five. The kiosk is at six, five. The first part affected only the horizontal coordinate, and the second affected only the vertical coordinate. Each step agrees with the grid.

From the kiosk at six, five, travel two units left. The first coordinate decreases from six to four while the second stays five. The garden is at four, five. Starting this new step from the original starting point would describe a different journey.

A learner starts every instruction from the origin. That can find several separate points, but it does not follow one continuous route. Each new movement starts where the previous movement ended. Draw connected arrows and keep the most recent ordered pair visible to avoid this mistake.

The courier travelled four units right, three units up and two units left. Add those distances to obtain nine units travelled. The destination alone does not show that total. Returning partway along a direction still adds distance to the route, even when it reduces the first coordinate.

The courier is at four, five and wants to return to two, two. Predict one route using only horizontal and vertical moves. Decide how much each coordinate must decrease. More than one order of instructions may work, so prepare to justify yours using the actual starting point.

Move two units left from four, five to two, five. Then move three units down to two, two. This return route has length five units. Moving down first and left second would also reach home on this unobstructed grid, but the intermediate point would be different.

A destination is one location. A route is a sequence of movements leading there. A detour can increase the total distance without changing the destination. When comparing two plans, check both the ordered pairs and the distances rather than assuming every path to the same point is equally long.

Our first-quadrant map does not show positions left of the vertical axis or below the horizontal axis. Check a planned move before taking it. If it would leave the displayed region, revise the instructions or explain the limitation. Do not silently wrap a counter to the opposite side.

Tell your partner the starting pair, each direction and distance, and the intended destination. Ask the partner to draw the route without seeing your version. Comparing the two drawings is a useful test of communication. If they differ, identify the first instruction where the paths separate.

A route goes three right and then three left from two, two. A student says nothing happened because the destination is unchanged. Is that a complete description? Decide the destination and total distance separately, and explain what the counter actually did during the journey.

The counter returns to two, two, but it travelled six units altogether: three out and three back. Zero change in position does not mean zero distance travelled. Recording the intermediate point five, two makes the movement visible and supports the explanation.
