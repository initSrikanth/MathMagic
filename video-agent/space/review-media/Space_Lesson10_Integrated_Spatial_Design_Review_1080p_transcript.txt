Our learning goal is to combine checked nets, routes and transformations in one design. We want to explain why a result is correct, as well as obtain the result. As you watch, look for the evidence that would help you convince a partner who has not seen your working.

Here are our three success criteria. I can justify a valid net. I can communicate a checked route. I can verify transformations and properties. We will check these ideas in two worked examples, investigate a common mistake and finish with an independent challenge. A good explanation links what you see in the model with the mathematical rule being used.

Use card for the kiosk, squared paper for the map and tracing paper for the sign. Each material helps answer a different question. A card model checks whether panels close. A grid checks location and direction. Tracing helps compare a shape with its translated, reflected or rotated image.

Choose the six-square cross as a cube kiosk model. All six faces are equal squares, each fold joins a whole matching edge, and the panels occupy different positions when closed. A successful paper fold supports the design. A colourful drawing without a fold check would provide weaker evidence.

The delivery depot starts at two, two on our unit grid. Plot a dot at the intersection rather than in the middle of a cell. Read across first and then up. Giving the planner this starting pair makes the later route reproducible instead of depending on a vague location description.

The courier moves four units right, three units up and then two units left. Predict the final coordinates. Write an intermediate pair after each instruction so you can check the route piece by piece. Keep the original depot point visible while tracking the latest position.

Four right gives six, two. Three up gives six, five. Two left gives four, five, the kiosk location. The courier travelled nine units in total. The final coordinates describe a position, while the sum of the movement distances describes the route length. Both checks belong in the plan.

A separate triangular garden motif must slide three right and two up. Every vertex follows the same movement. For example, one, one becomes four, three. Check the other vertices too. The garden shape keeps its side lengths, angles and orientation, even though its location on the park map changes.

A sketch can look almost correct while one vertex is a grid unit out of place. Check numerical relationships against the drawing. A net needs matching edges, a route needs correct coordinate changes, and a transformation needs its own invariant rules. Appearance alone cannot establish all three.

A sign point starts at two, three. First slide it two units right. Then reflect its new position in the vertical mirror through five. These instructions describe a sequence. The input to the reflection is the result of the slide, so keep that intermediate point in the record.

Start at two, three, slide two right, then reflect across the vertical line through five. Predict the final coordinates. Which point is actually being reflected? Measure its distance from the mirror after the slide, rather than measuring from the original point.

The slide moves two, three to four, three. That point lies one unit left of the mirror. Its reflection lies one unit right, at six, three. The completed sequence therefore sends the original sign point to six, three. The intermediate pair explains how we obtained the answer.

Try reflecting the original two, three first. It becomes eight, three. Sliding that result two right gives ten, three. This differs from six, three, so the two operations cannot simply be exchanged. The example shows why a design must communicate the order of transformations clearly.

Our kiosk cube belongs to the rectangular-prism family. Six faces alone would not identify it, because a long carton has six faces too. All six cube faces are squares, and its edges have equal lengths. A classification statement should name the properties supporting the label.

A useful review checks each claim separately. Does the kiosk close? Are landmarks at the stated coordinates? Do route steps reach their labelled destinations? Does each transformation follow its named line, centre or movement? Separate checks help locate an error without discarding the parts of the plan that already work.

Create a small park map with three landmarks, a checked kiosk net and a transformed motif. Ask a partner to reproduce one route and one transformation from your written instructions. What evidence would convince you that your plan is precise, rather than merely attractive?

Compare your partner’s results with your intended map. If something differs, find the first step where the evidence separates. Correct that instruction and test again. Strong spatial reasoning connects models, coordinates and properties, and explains why a result works. It also treats a discovered error as useful information for improvement.
