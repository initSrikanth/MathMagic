Our learning goal is to design, test and improve a net for packaging. We want to explain why a result is correct, as well as obtain the result. As you watch, look for the evidence that would help you convince a partner who has not seen your working.

Here are our three success criteria. I can plan the required faces. I can check dimensions and joins. I can explain a useful repair. We will check these ideas in two worked examples, investigate a common mistake and finish with an independent challenge. A good explanation links what you see in the model with the mathematical rule being used.

Use scrap paper before cutting expensive card. Draw fold lines, label each panel and mark the intended base. Cut only around the outside with adult help when needed. Gently fold along shared edges. Testing a small prototype helps you find errors while changes are still easy.

Imagine a rectangular box two units long, one unit wide and one unit high. It needs two long horizontal faces, two long side faces, and two square end faces. Opposite faces match. Listing the required faces gives us a useful check before we choose an arrangement.

Our box needs four rectangles measuring two units by one unit and two squares measuring one by one. A shared fold edge must have the same length on both panels. Equal numbers of panels do not help if one intended join is longer on one side than the other.

Look at the band of four rectangles. The two square end faces must close opposite openings when the band wraps around. Should both squares try to cover the same end? Predict where to place them, then explain which opening each square is meant to close.

The four rectangles wrap into the four long faces of the box. Attach one square to each side of the band so they close opposite ends. The short edges match the square sides. We have accounted for every face and assigned each panel a different place on the solid.

A fold line joins two faces that remain connected in the flat design. A cut separates edges that will meet later when the package closes. Marking these differently helps someone else build your design. A glue tab lies outside a face and supports a join; it does not change the face count.

A two by three rectangle of six squares contains enough square material for a cube, but it does not fold into one correctly. Do not repair it by stretching a face. Keep equal squares and change the connection pattern. The geometry of the intended cube must remain unchanged.

Place four equal squares in a straight column. Attach one square on each side of the second square. Check that every square can be reached across a shared whole edge. This connected cross gives the base four immediate neighbours and leaves the final square available as a lid.

Compare the invalid rectangle with the cross. Can you explain why moving panels changes the result even though the total number of squares stays six? Predict the role of the last square in the cross. Use face positions rather than saying that one drawing just looks better.

Fold the cross mentally or with paper. The base stays still, four neighbouring squares form walls, and the last square closes the top. The repair works because the six panels occupy six different face positions. A successful test explains the change; it is more than a neat final drawing.

Label an outside edge before folding and find the edge that meets it after folding. Their lengths must match. Repeat around the opening. This catches a wrongly sized lid even when the overall arrangement is sensible. A gap can come from a missing face or from an incorrect dimension.

A useful design record says what failed, what changed and why the change should help. For example, two panels competed for the same face position, so we moved one to cover the empty top. This explanation lets another designer reproduce and evaluate your repair.

Colouring or rotating the whole net does not fix its fold relationships. Those changes affect the page view, but do not change which faces share edges. A genuine geometric repair changes a dimension or connection responsible for the failure while preserving the intended solid.

Your partner says a box net is correct because opposite faces have matching dimensions. Is that enough evidence? Think about what else a flat design must do when it folds. Prepare a short checklist a younger designer could use without already knowing the answer.

Check the required faces, the lengths of meeting edges, and the folded positions. All three matter. A net can have the right shapes and dimensions but still overlap. A prototype that closes correctly, together with a clear explanation, gives much stronger evidence than any single check alone.
