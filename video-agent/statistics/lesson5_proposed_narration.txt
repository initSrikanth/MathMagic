Your class can choose one library display theme. How can we make a recommendation that listens to the class? Imagine a display table waiting for a new library theme. A bright idea is useful, but the class should have a voice in the choice. We can use an investigation to move from a question to evidence, and from evidence to a recommendation that explains its limits.

Today we are learning to conduct an investigation and communicate a careful conclusion. Our goal is to complete an investigation and communicate a careful conclusion. We will connect the skills from the earlier lessons. The final report should explain the question, who answered, how responses were checked, what the display shows and which decision the evidence can support.

I can pose a useful question and collect relevant checked data. I can choose a display and interpret its mode and shape. I can report evidence, a limitation and a sensible next question. Recall the different jobs we have practised. A fair question collects relevant information. A frequency table counts checked responses. A column graph compares category counts. The mode identifies a most common choice. Now we will bring those pieces together, with context and a next step.

Use a cycle: question → plan → collect → check → display → interpret → communicate. Name the group, keep the collection fair and record one anonymous response per pupil. Choose a column graph for named choices, ordered columns for ratings or counts, and a line graph for change over time. A sample tells us about those asked; do not automatically speak for the whole school. Begin by naming the decision and the group. Our question asks which library theme this class would choose. The allowed choices are Animals, Space, Sport and Other. Club names and theme names are nominal categories: they have no natural order. A column graph will compare frequencies; a line joining themes would wrongly suggest a time sequence.

We want to know which theme our class prefers: animals, space or sport. Is “You love space, don’t you?” a fair survey? Before collecting anything, compare the two possible survey questions. Does either one suggest that a particular answer is expected? Think about how you would feel if your favourite were different. Choose wording that lets pupils answer honestly, and keep your reason ready.

Ask “Which of these themes would you choose?” using the same options for everyone, with another option too. Ask all class members once, allow a private response and record no names in the report. A collection plan should say when the class will respond, how each pupil will be counted once, and what happens if someone is away. Use the same choices for everyone. Let pupils answer privately and record anonymous choices in the final data. Checking the collection method matters as much as drawing the graph afterwards.

Ten classmates vote: Animals 4, Space 3, Sport 2, Other 1. Which recommendation is supported? Now sort ten fictional practice responses. Four choose Animals, three choose Space, two choose Sport and one chooses Other. Before recommending anything, predict the modal theme and the total number of votes. Ask yourself whether the leading choice must also have more than half of all the votes.

Animals is the modal choice: 4 of 10 votes. It leads, but 6 classmates chose something else. Recommend an animals display for this class while noting that absent pupils were not asked. Ask them before a final decision; do not claim the whole school agrees. Count the frequencies together: four plus three plus two plus one gives ten. Animals has the highest frequency, but four is less than half of ten. Six pupils chose another option. The evidence supports saying Animals leads among these ten replies. It does not support saying every pupil agrees, or that most of the whole school chose Animals.

“The most popular choice is a majority.” A majority is more than half. Four out of ten leads this vote but is not more than half. A leading category can be useful for a decision even when it is not a majority. We can recommend an Animals display while acknowledging other preferences. Perhaps the class could rotate themes later. That is a possible decision based on the evidence, rather than a new fact proved by the graph.

Pause the player and try the investigation in the written lesson. Make a prediction, collect or change the data, then explain what you notice. Consider a second collection. Only eight of twenty-four classmates reply: Art receives four votes, Sport three and Music one. Four leads among the eight replies, but sixteen pupils are missing. Ask them before claiming a complete class preference. Missing responses could change the distribution and the recommendation.

Use your investigation report to explain a decision, its evidence, who it applies to, and what you would check next. Build your report in a clear order. State the question and collection group. Describe one validation check. Give a frequency and total from the display. Explain your recommendation. Then name a limitation and a next step that could improve the evidence. A partner should be able to check every numerical claim against your table.

What would you investigate next if another class made a different choice? Different groups may have different preferences, and that variation is worth exploring. Keep your conclusions connected to the group and evidence you actually collected. Ask, check, display, interpret and communicate: those steps help you make a thoughtful decision.
