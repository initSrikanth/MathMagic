Now we also need to notice whether the groups have an order, and whether a number is a count or just a label.

Data are pieces of information.

Nominal categories are names with no natural order, such as art, sport and music.

Ordinal categories have an order, such as low, medium and high interest.

Discrete numerical data are counts, such as the number of library books borrowed.

A statistical question expects different answers across a group.

Imagine handing out three different cards.

The first card names a club: art, sport or music.

We can arrange those names in any order without changing their meaning.

The second card records low, medium or high interest.

That order matters.

The third card asks for a count of visits.

Which responses could be different from person to person?

This asks about a group and expects variation.

Ask every Year 5 class using the same options, once per pupil.

Include another option so pupils are not forced into a poor match.

Notice that a question about the colour of one particular ball can be answered by looking at that ball once.

Our club question is different.

Different pupils may choose different clubs.

That variation is useful, because it helps us understand the group instead of assuming everyone agrees.

We record club choice, interest level and number of club visits last term.

Which can be put in order?

What would change if another pupil had not visited at all?

Club choice is nominal.

Interest level is ordinal: low, medium, high.

Visits are discrete numbers: 0, 1, 2 and so on.

A pupil number is a label, not a count of something.

A count of two visits tells us how many visits happened.

High interest tells us an ordered category, rather than an exact amount.

Music names a choice, without saying it is above or below art.

We can count responses in any of these groups, but counting responses does not turn club names into numerical measurements.

“Numbers always mean numerical data.” A sports shirt number is a name label.

Ask what the value describes, not just how it looks.

Imagine a football shirt with the number ten.

Adding the shirt numbers would not tell us how many pupils play football.

Those numbers identify shirts.

A count of ten pupils answers a different question.

The meaning of a number is more important than its appearance.

Ask the same neutral question in every Year 5 class.

Record one response per pupil, include another-club option, and decide what to do about pupils who are absent.

You do not need names on the final graph.

A checked class list can prevent accidental double counting without making names public.

Write a question for a school decision.

Name the group you will ask, the variable you will collect and how you will avoid counting anyone twice.

Check your plan with these questions.

Could the wording push pupils towards one answer?

Could a whole class be missing?

Could the same pupil be counted twice?

Explain one change that would make the plan fairer.

A useful investigation begins before the first response is collected.
