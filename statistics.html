YEAR 5 • STATISTICS

Data Detectives — Statistics

Ask • check • explore • track • investigate

LESSON 1

Ask a question worth investigating

Investigate first

Our school wants a new lunchtime club. Would asking only your football team tell us what all Year 5 pupils want?

Today’s Learning Goal

Plan a fair way to collect useful data.

Success Criteria: I can…

  • Tell names, ordered groups and counts apart.
  • Pose a question that allows different answers.
  • Choose who to ask and how to record one response each.

Build the idea

Data are pieces of information. Nominal categories are names with no natural order, such as art, sport and music. Ordinal categories have an order, such as low, medium and high interest. Discrete numerical data are counts, such as the number of library books borrowed. A statistical question expects different answers across a group.

Guided example 1: Predict before opening

Which club would each Year 5 pupil choose: art, sport, music or another club?

Compare your thinking

This asks about a group and expects variation. Ask every Year 5 class using the same options, once per pupil. Include another option so pupils are not forced into a poor match.

Guided example 2: Sort the evidence

We record club choice, interest level and number of club visits last term. Which can be put in order?

Compare your thinking

Club choice is nominal. Interest level is ordinal: low, medium, high. Visits are discrete numbers: 0, 1, 2 and so on. A pupil number is a label, not a count of something.

Try an investigation

Choose a type for each variable. Predict first, then test your classification.

Check a common mistake

“Numbers always mean numerical data.” A sports shirt number is a name label. Ask what the value describes, not just how it looks.

Reflection and exit

Write a question for a school decision. Name the group you will ask, the variable you will collect and how you will avoid counting anyone twice.

LESSON 2

Check, count and display

Investigate first

The canteen has a list of snack votes, but “apple” and “Apple” appear as different choices. Will the graph tell the right story?

Today’s Learning Goal

Turn checked responses into a trustworthy frequency display.

Success Criteria: I can…

  • Spot missing, invalid and repeated records.
  • Make a frequency table that counts every valid response once.
  • Use a digital table or spreadsheet to represent data.

Build the idea

Frequency means how often a category or value occurs. Validate means check the data against the question and collection rules before counting. Use a consistent spelling, check missing answers with the collector, and investigate duplicate pupil records. Matching answers from different pupils are not duplicates. Never quietly invent a missing answer.

Guided example 1: Predict the total

Six different pupils answered: Apple, Banana, apple, Pear, Apple, Banana. How many valid votes are there?

Compare your thinking

Standardise apple to Apple: Apple 3, Banana 2, Pear 1. The total is 3 + 2 + 1 = 6. All six votes count; repeated snack choices are normal.

Guided example 2: Use a spreadsheet

Put one vote in each cell A2 to A7. In C2 type Apple. What does =COUNTIF(A2:A7,C2) find?

Compare your thinking

It counts the cells matching Apple. Use a row for each category and check that the frequencies sum to six. A column graph has separated equal-width bars, category labels and a frequency scale starting at zero.

Try an investigation

One response per pupil, separated by commas. Allowed choices: Apple, Banana, Pear. Try a spelling error or a blank entry, then repair it. Repeated choices count.

Spreadsheet connection: enter one vote per cell and use =COUNTIF(A2:A7,C2) to count the category in C2.

Check a common mistake

“Remove every repeated answer.” Only repeated records for the same pupil need checking. Different pupils may choose the same snack.

Reflection and exit

Explain one check you made before counting and one way to check the finished table.

LESSON 3

Read the whole distribution

Investigate first

Two classes both have a mode of two library books. Must their borrowing patterns look the same?

Today’s Learning Goal

Describe the mode and shape of a data distribution in context.

Success Criteria: I can…

  • Identify the most frequent value or category.
  • Describe clusters, gaps and how frequencies are spread.
  • Compare two distributions using evidence.

Build the idea

The mode is the value or category with the highest frequency, not the frequency itself. Two values can tie for the highest frequency. If all observed values occur equally often, there is no useful mode. A distribution shows how all the responses are spread. Describe where most values gather, any gaps and whether counts are balanced or trail to one side. Keep zero-frequency counts to show gaps.

Guided example 1: Predict the mode

Books borrowed: 0, 1, 2, 2, 2, 3, 4. What is the mode, and what does it mean?

Compare your thinking

Two books is the mode because three pupils borrowed two books. Most values gather around two; the frequencies on either side are balanced. The mode is 2 books, not 3 pupils.

Guided example 2: Same mode, different story

Class A frequencies for 0,1,2,3,4 books: 1,2,5,2,1. Class B: 0,0,5,4,2. What stays the same?

Compare your thinking

Both have a mode of 2 books. A is balanced around 2. B has no pupils at 0 or 1 and all values are 2 to 4. A shared mode does not describe the whole distribution. If two bars tie at the top, report both modes.

Try an investigation

Change the number of pupils at each book count. Predict how the mode and shape will change, then update the display.

Check a common mistake

“The mode describes everyone.” It describes the most common value. Look at all the bars before making a claim about a group.

Reflection and exit

Change the frequencies in the explorer. Describe a cluster or gap and explain why the mode alone misses part of the story.

LESSON 4

Follow change over time

Investigate first

The school garden tank is filling. A rising line looks promising, but can we promise what the tank will hold tomorrow?

Today’s Learning Goal

Interpret line graphs and explain what their evidence supports.

Success Criteria: I can…

  • Read the time axis and the labelled scale.
  • Compare changes over equal time intervals.
  • Separate observations from uncertain predictions.

Build the idea

A line graph shows observations in time order. The horizontal axis shows time and the vertical axis shows the measured quantity and unit. Equal spaces must mean equal time intervals. Read the scale before comparing heights. A rising segment means an increase, a falling one a decrease and a flat one no change. The line connects observations; it does not prove every value between them or any future value.

Guided example 1: Predict the biggest rise

A tank holds 10, 20, 20 and 40 litres on Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday and Thursday. Which equal daily interval rose most?

Compare your thinking

Changes are +10 L, 0 L and +20 L. Wednesday to Thursday rose most. The highest point and the largest rise answer different questions.

Guided example 2: Compare carefully

Tank A: 10,20,20,40 L. Tank B: 20,20,30,30 L on the same days. Which is fuller on Thursday? Can we promise Friday?

Compare your thinking

A has 40 L and B has 30 L on Thursday, a difference of 10 L. We need new observations for Friday: rain and water use may change. If a vertical axis starts above zero, a small change can look dramatic; read the labels.

Try an investigation

Move Thursday’s tank reading. Predict which daily interval will have the biggest rise. Compare the two tanks at the same time.

Check a common mistake

“A rising line must keep rising.” It records what happened during the shown times. A forecast is uncertain and needs extra evidence.

Reflection and exit

Describe one interval using its two days and change in litres. Write one claim the graph cannot prove.

LESSON 5

Make a decision with evidence

Investigate first

Your class can choose one library display theme. How can we make a recommendation that listens to the class?

Today’s Learning Goal

Conduct an investigation and communicate a careful conclusion.

Success Criteria: I can…

  • Pose a useful question and collect relevant checked data.
  • Choose a display and interpret its mode and shape.
  • Report evidence, a limitation and a sensible next question.

Build the idea

Use a cycle: question → plan → collect → check → display → interpret → communicate. Name the group, keep the collection fair and record one anonymous response per pupil. Choose a column graph for named choices, ordered columns for ratings or counts, and a line graph for change over time. A sample tells us about those asked; do not automatically speak for the whole school.

Guided example 1: Design before collecting

We want to know which theme our class prefers: animals, space or sport. Is “You love space, don’t you?” a fair survey?

Compare your thinking

Ask “Which of these themes would you choose?” using the same options for everyone, with another option too. Ask all class members once, allow a private response and record no names in the report.

Guided example 2: Make the evidence useful

Ten classmates vote: Animals 4, Space 3, Sport 2, Other 1. Which recommendation is supported?

Compare your thinking

Animals is the modal choice: 4 of 10 votes. It leads, but 6 classmates chose something else. Recommend an animals display for this class while noting that absent pupils were not asked. Ask them before a final decision; do not claim the whole school agrees.

Try an investigation

Investigate your class’s preferred library theme. Use anonymous responses from classmates, or load the clearly labelled practice data. Collect one vote each; include Other. No names are needed.

Check a common mistake

“The most popular choice is a majority.” A majority is more than half. Four out of ten leads this vote but is not more than half.

Reflection and exit

Use your investigation report to explain a decision, its evidence, who it applies to, and what you would check next.
